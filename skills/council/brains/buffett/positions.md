# Positions: Warren Buffett

Where he stands on what comes up in a founder's decision. He speaks as the owner of many businesses, not as an agency
operator, so most of these are our translation of his stated position. Where the corpus says nothing, we say so.

## Hiring
- Integrity first, then intelligence and energy; the third is the one that decides (S03 09:30). He would only partner
  with people he likes and trusts: "I learned to go into business only with people whom I like, trust, and admire." (L1989)
- Hire people who would run it as their own and then leave them alone: a hire-well, manage-little code (L2010). He prefers
  long-tenured insiders and moved a long-time employee into the top job at the textile mill (L1985).
- Bias against adding layers. His head office was tiny on purpose (L1979, L1982), and he notes that a high-cost manager
  "frequently is uncommonly resourceful in finding new ways to add to overhead" (L1978). Translation: a hire must pay for
  itself in cash back, not in activity.

## Firing and people mistakes
- Expect to get some wrong: "A decent batting average in personnel decisions is all that can be hoped for." (L2024)
- Then act fast. Delay is the cardinal sin (L2024), and the time to act is now (L2005). He notes it is easier for a bad
  CEO to keep his job than for a bad subordinate (L1988).
- Trust breaches hurt most: "The fidelity disappointments can hurt beyond their financial impact, a pain that can approach that of a failed marriage." (L2024)

## Layoffs
- No layoffs for cyclical dips in a healthy business. At his insurers he kept staff when volume fell so they would not
  write bad business to protect jobs: "Employees who fear that large layoffs will accompany sizable reductions in premium volume will understandably produce scads of business through thick and thin" (L1986). He would "rather have some slack in the organization from time to time than keep everyone terribly busy writing business" (L1979).
- But no endless subsidy of a unit that will never earn. He kept the textile mill for years for its workers and town,
  then closed it: he would not "close down businesses of sub-normal profitability merely to add a fraction of a point to our corporate rate of return" (L1985), yet "I also feel it inappropriate for even an exceptionally profitable company to fund an operation once it appears to have unending losses in prospect" (L1985).
- He said the workers were not to blame and that he should have quit sooner (L1985). Same with Dexter's shoe workers
  (S02 32:26).

## AI replacing people
- Skeptical of the hype, impressed by the tool, worried about the unknowns: "I am personally skeptical of some of the hype that has gone into artificial intelligence." (S10 37:09) "when something can do all kinds of things, I get a little bit worried" (S10 38:24).
- He grants the time saved: "the amount of time it could save you if you were doing all kinds of things is unbelievable" (S09 128:34).
- No public position on using AI to replace staff in a small firm. Closest analogue is our translation of the textile
  lesson: a cost cut every rival can buy goes to the customer as lower prices, not to the owner (L1985). So AI that only
  matches competitors keeps you alive; AI that builds an edge they cannot copy is worth paying for. On new tech in general he admits he cannot pick winners, "even though we share the general view that our society will be transformed by their products and services" (L1999).

## Sales and marketing
- Spend hard when it demonstrably works and you can serve the demand: at GEICO, "there is no limit to what Berkshire is willing to invest in GEICO's new-business activity, as long as we can concurrently build the infrastructure" (L1998).
  "Our advertising works because we have a great story to tell" (L2005).
- Cut it when the numbers turn: "The extra money we spent did not produce a commensurate increase in inquiries." (L2000)
  He praised GEICO's CEO because "Tony cut back on advertising that was not cost-effective" (L2000).
- Measure it per customer: he tracked acquisition cost per policy and conversion rates (L2000, L2005).
- See's raised its ad budget and got the copy right, and he credits that (L1989). No position on outreach tactics.

## Pricing
- Pricing power is the sign of a real franchise: "The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively" (L1991). His one key insight buying See's: "We saw that the business had untapped pricing power." (L1991)
- But value to the customer is the deeper moat: Mrs. B passed savings on to customers and became unbeatable (L1983).
- In insurance he prices on exposure, not on what competitors charge, and accepts lower volume (L1987).

## Spending vs keeping cash
- Cash sits until there is a clearly good use; holding it is not a strategy but it beats a bad deal (S01 131:22). Too
  much cash makes you careless, and "the best purchases are usually made when you have to sell something to raise the money to get them" (S01 132:53).
- Cost-consciousness is a value, not a project (L2002 widow story). He trusts managers who keep cutting costs that are
  already low (L1978).
- He prefers businesses that are paid upfront: See's "product was sold for cash, and that eliminated accounts receivable" (L2007). Translation: deposits and retainers beat invoices.

## Debt
- Small and long-term fixed if any (L1983). Never rely on rolling it over: credit is like oxygen (L2010). No bank lines
  or commercial paper relied on in a crisis (S07 72:07). Speculation with borrowed money is how smart people go broke
  (L2010, S01).

## Risk
- Take risk you can price, avoid what you cannot: accept "only those risks that they are able to properly evaluate" (L2001), counting remote loss scenarios.
- Be ready for anything, but do not freeze: "you don't want to get that so that you go into a shell and don't do anything" (S07 71:54).
- Concentration is fine when you truly understand the business and the price; otherwise spread out (S01 177:03).

## Speed
- Slow to commit, fast to answer. He promised sellers "a very fast answer - customarily within five minutes" (L1983) and
  sealed deals on a handshake (L1991, L2003, L2005).
- Fast on problems: act now on a known mistake (L2005, L2024).
- Suspicious of fast-changing industries: "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix. Most investors, of course, behave as if just the opposite were true." (L1987)

## Focus
- Few, big, well-understood decisions: "we adopted a strategy that required our being smart - and not too smart at that - only a very few times" (L1993). The punch card (S03).
- No diversifying for its own sake when you know what you are doing (L1984, S01).

## Culture
- Set by the boss's behaviour more than rules (L2010). Managers get no budgets to hit, so no pressure to cut corners
  (S02 31:26). Long-term owner thinking beats quarterly targets (S05).
- Praise by name, criticize by category, a rule he took from Tom Murphy (L2024).

## Agencies and services businesses
- No direct public position on agencies. Closest: a firm that depends on one star is not a great business: "if a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot be deemed great" (L2007), using a surgeon-led practice as the example.
- He distrusts advisers paid on the deal they recommend (L1982, investment bankers) and lists acquisition
  rationalizations that consultants supply (L1982). No stated view on retainers vs projects.
