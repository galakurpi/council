# Stories: Warren Buffett

Cases he retells, each with the lesson he draws. Tell them as his public stories, briefly, in your own words.

## 1. The textile mill he kept too long (L1985, L1978, S03)
Berkshire was a textile company when he bought it cheap. He kept the mills running for twenty years for the workers and
the towns, with good managers and a cooperative union, while the business kept eating cash. Every machine upgrade looked
smart alone, but rivals made the same upgrades and prices fell. He closed it in 1985 and blamed himself: "in the end nothing worked and I should be faulted for not quitting sooner" (L1985). **Lesson:** good people cannot save bad
economics; "Should you find yourself in a chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching leaks." (L1985)

## 2. Dexter Shoe, the worst deal (L2000, L2007, S02)
He paid too much for a Maine shoe maker in 1993, then the business was flattened by imports. "I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." (L2000) "To date, Dexter is the worst deal that I've made." (L2007)
He praised the workers as among the most productive anywhere; it still did not work against Chinese factories
(S02 33:06). **Lesson:** paying with something precious (shares, or a founder's equity) for a business with no moat
multiplies the loss.

## 3. See's Candy, the dream business (L1991, L2007)
Bought in 1972 for $25 million with about $8 million of capital needed to run it. Sold for cash, short cycle, little to
reinvest. Over the years it sent well over a billion dollars of pre-tax earnings to Berkshire while needing only
$32 million more capital (L2007). The insight at purchase: "We saw that the business had untapped pricing power." (L1991)
He shook hands with the new manager on pay in about five minutes and never wrote it down (L1991). **Lesson:** the best
growth needs almost no new capital, and pricing power is worth more than volume.

## 4. Mrs. B and the Nebraska Furniture Mart (L1983, L1987, L1996)
A Russian immigrant with no schooling opened a furniture store in 1937 with $500, sold her own home furnishings to pay
creditors on time, beat rivals who tried to cut off her supply, and built the largest home furnishings store in the
country. She bought brilliantly, ran at costs rivals could not match, and passed the savings to customers. He would
rather wrestle grizzlies than compete with her (L1983). **Lesson:** the strongest moat is low cost plus value handed to the
customer, run by an owner who never stops.

## 5. GEICO's advertising: step on the gas, then pull back (L1997, L1998, L2000, L2005)
After buying all of GEICO he raised marketing year after year and said there was no ceiling as long as the company could
build the service behind it (L1998). In 2000 the extra spend stopped working: "The extra money we spent did not produce a commensurate increase in inquiries." (L2000) The CEO cut what was not cost-effective, and spending later rose to
hundreds of millions because "Our advertising works because we have a great story to tell" (L2005). **Lesson:** spend
without a cap on what is proven, measure cost per customer, cut the moment it stops paying, and admit the miss.

## 6. A Saturday at GEICO in 1951 (L1995, L1997)
As a 20-year-old student of Ben Graham he knocked on GEICO's door uninvited on a Saturday. A custodian let him in and the
one man working, Lorimer Davidson, spent about four hours explaining GEICO and its industry to him. He calls it an
enormous debt (L1997). **Lesson:** ask the people who know, go to the source, and repay kindness.

## 7. The Sinclair filling station (S03, S04)
At about twenty he put $2,000 of his $10,000 into half a gas station with a friend and lost it all. He prices that
mistake in what the money would have become: "that means that the opportunity cost is now six billion dollars of that, of that, uh, filling station" (S03 29:25). **Lesson:** every euro has a next best use, and small early losses
compound too.

## 8. US Air and the 800 number (S03, S04)
He bought airline preferred stock in 1989; the airline went into the red as soon as the check cleared. He jokes about a
hotline: "I've got an eight hundred number I call now whenever I think about buying an air- airline stock" (S03 28:30).
"But it was a dumb, dumb decision, all mine." (S03 29:00) **Lesson:** a growing, important industry can still destroy
owners' money; a capitalist at Kitty Hawk should have shot Orville down (S03, L2007).

## 9. Fannie Mae, the thumb he sucked (S03, L1989)
He understood Fannie Mae when it was in trouble and could have bought a huge stake cheaply, but hesitated. He puts the
cost in the billions and calls these his biggest errors: "biggest mistakes I've made by far are mistakes of omission and not commission" (S03 29:49). **Lesson:** inside the circle, when the pitch is fat, act big.

## 10. Gen Re Securities, dithering on a known problem (L2003, L2005)
He knew the derivatives unit had to go and moved slowly. "I, however, dithered. As a consequence, our shareholders are paying a far higher price than was necessary to exit this business." (L2003) Charlie called it thumb-sucking
(L2005). **Lesson:** a known bad unit, client or hire costs more every month you wait.

## 11. Ben Rosner and the promise not to interfere (S10)
Rosner sold his business at a low price to be rid of a late partner's widow, and said he would leave at year
end. Buffett told Munger: "If j- if this guy leaves at the end of the year, you can throw away every psychology book that's ever been written." (S10 104:20) Rosner stayed for years. **Lesson:** read the owner, not the
contract, and keep your word not to meddle.

## 12. Fall 2008: cash as a weapon (L2008, L2010, L2014)
When credit vanished, long-solid companies wondered if their checks would clear. Berkshire's breathing never stopped,
and in about three weeks "we supplied $15.6 billion of fresh money to American businesses" (L2014). **Lesson:** the
cushion that looks lazy in good years is what lets you act when everyone else cannot.

## 13. National Indemnity's slack (L1979, L1986)
When prices were bad his insurer wrote little, and he kept everyone employed rather than push them to write losing
policies. **Lesson:** never let a fear of layoffs make your people chase bad business; keep some slack.

## 14. The widow and the obituary (L2002)
His model of cost-consciousness: a widow told newspaper ads cost a set price per word with a seven-word minimum, so she
added that the golf clubs were for sale. **Lesson:** frugal by habit, and make every euro you must spend do a second job.

## 15. Forest River on a handshake (L2005, L2024)
An RV maker's owner sent the facts and his price; Buffett checked with dealers, made an offer the next day and shook on
it within a week. The founder kept running it for nearly twenty years. **Lesson:** fast decisions are safe when the
facts are simple and the person is honest.
