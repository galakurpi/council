# Frameworks: Alex Hormozi

The models he actually runs, book first (B1 to B4), then how he applies them on camera (S01 to S46). Ids point to
`sources.md`. Each one says what it is, when to reach for it, and how to turn it into a 1 to 10 score for an option.
Scores are this seat's convention, built from his own thresholds; he does not score on a 1 to 10 scale himself.

## 1. The Value Equation (B1, ch. 6 "Value Offer: The Value Equation")

- **What:** value = (dream outcome x perceived likelihood of achievement) / (perceived time delay x perceived effort and
  sacrifice). Two levers go up, two go down. It is a division on purpose: if the bottom reaches zero, value is unlimited.
  Perception is what counts, not reality (the London Tube map cut perceived waiting more than faster trains would).
  His father's four questions map onto it: what will I make, how will I know it works, how long, what do I have to do.
- **What he learned over time:** beginners inflate the top (bigger claims). The best companies win on the bottom: faster
  and easier. "Many will always be willing to pay (price) for the (value) of speed" (B1). Fast beats free. A speed premium
  is pure margin: +50% for 24-hour delivery, +300% for 60 minutes, same work (S15 19:55); a 20% "priority boost" to jump
  the queue (S42 18:40).
- **Likelihood is proof:** the 10,000th surgery is worth more than the first. Guarantees, track record, case studies and
  proof near the prospect raise it. "if you're trying to sell anyone anything, um, proof will always beat promise" (S18 90:01).
- **Use:** any "why won't they buy", any repricing, any offer change, any AI-or-human delivery question (does it make the
  outcome faster or easier for the buyer?).
- **Score:** rate the option on each of the four levers against today (-2 to +2 each), add them, map to 1-10 (0 = 5).
  An option that only cuts price moves nothing in the equation and scores 3 or lower. An option that shortens time to the
  first win (a $2,000 sale in the client's first seven days, B1) scores high even with no new features.

## 2. Grand Slam Offer: build it, then enhance it (B1, ch. 2, 8-16)

- **What:** an offer "so good they would feel stupid saying no". B1 defines the offer as more than the product: "the offer
  is the goods and services you agree to give or provide, how you accept payment, and the terms of the agreement" (B1).
- **Build (ch. 9-10), five steps:** (1) name the dream outcome, the vacation not the plane flight; (2) list every problem
  in the order the customer hits it, before, during and after (each problem has four forms, one per value lever, so 16 core
  problems with sub-problems); (3) turn each problem into a "how to" solution; (4) list every delivery vehicle using his
  cheat codes: 1-on-1, small group or one-to-many; DIY, done-with-you or done-for-you; medium; response speed; and the
  10x / one-tenth price test; (5) trim and stack: cut high cost low value first, then low cost low value. "What should
  remain are offer items that are 1) low cost, high value and 2) high cost, high value" (B1). One-to-many tools have the
  widest gap between cost and value (his 100-hour meal-plan spreadsheet sold as personalised plans that took 15 minutes).
- **Enhance (ch. 11-16):** scarcity (limited quantity: total cap, growth-rate cap, cohort cap), urgency (limited time:
  rolling cohorts, seasonal, price or bonus deadlines, exploding offers), bonuses (each solves a named obstacle, each with
  a name, proof and a price tag; tools and checklists beat more training), guarantees (unconditional, conditional,
  anti-guarantee, implied/performance), naming (M-A-G-I-C: magnet, avatar, goal, interval, container; renaming the same
  offer re-stimulates demand).
- **Guarantee maths:** "If you close 130 percent as many people, and your refund percentage doubles from 5 percent to 10
  percent, you've still made 1.23x the money" (B1). High delivery costs push you to conditional or anti-guarantees.
- **Later refinements on camera:** each bonus should be worth more than the whole core product, a bonus that needs
  explaining gets deleted, and the offer should be "textable" (S27 63:28, 64:30). Trim to the one thing that gets them
  over the hump (S30 13:54). In sales, add a bonus to close instead of discounting (S10 25:03).
- **Use:** before buying traffic, before hiring sales, whenever close rates or response rates are low.
- **Score:** would the target buyer say "all that, for only that?" Count the obstacles it solves out of those listed
  (share solved x 10), minus 2 if it relies on a discount, minus 2 if it cannot be fulfilled at a margin you can live with.

## 3. Market first: Starving Crowd > Offer > Persuasion (B1, ch. 4)

- **What:** "Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills" (B1). A great market beats a bad offer; a
  normal market plus a great offer is the path for most; a normal offer in a normal market needs exceptional selling.
  Four market tests: massive pain, purchasing power, easy to target, growing. The resume-fixer who sold to the unemployed
  failed on purchasing power; Lloyd's newspaper software failed on growth (a market shrinking 25% a year).
- **Niche:** under $10M a year, narrower makes more money; the same time-management course goes from $19 to $1,000-2,000
  as it narrows to one rep type (Dan Kennedy's example). Gym Launch served only micro gyms doing weight loss: "a niche of a
  niche of a niche" still at $30M-plus (S08 54:48). Being stuck for years can mean the market is too small: his peers all
  had markets 10-100x his (S04 26:08).
- **Where the money is:** sell to people who can pay. "Solve rich people problems. They pay better" (S03 98:57). "The first
  reason that you aren't making as much as you want is because you're selling to people who don't have the money to give
  you" (S31 00:28). Narrow to the top 20% of customers by value (Vista's method, S08).
- **Score:** pain, money, targeting, growth, 0-2.5 each. A shrinking market caps the score at 4 whatever the offer.

## 4. Pricing: out of the commodity trap, then up the ladder (B1, ch. 3 and 5; S28, S31, S41)

- **Commodity vs category of one (B1, ch. 3):** if the buyer can say "these are the same", they buy the cheaper one. The
  Grand Slam Offer sells "in a vacuum". His agency-software example: 2.5x response x 2.5x close x 4x price up front, so
  spending $10,000 brings back $112,000 instead of losing half the ad spend.
- **Virtuous cycle of price (B1, ch. 5):** higher price raises client investment, results, perceived value and margin,
  which buys better people and service. "99 percent of businesses need to raise their prices to grow, not lower them" (B1).
  "Profit is oxygen. It fuels the fire of growth" (B1). On camera: price, margin, talent, service, reputation, demand,
  price again (S31, S43).
- **The close-rate ladder (S28 01:12, S18 16:34):** closing 80%+ means 3-4x underpriced; 60-80% means 2-3x; 50-60% means
  1.5-2x; 40-50% means 1.25-1.5x; 30-40% is about right if leads are educated before the call; below 30% is an avatar or
  sales-process problem, so fix that before touching price. "usually you're appropriately priced when seven out of 10
  people are saying no" (S18 16:34). Stop raising when conversion x price falls; a 50% raise that loses 10% of sales stays
  (S43 29:14). From zero: start free for proof, then raise 20% every five customers (S08 04:25, S16 28:45).
- **Price from cost for services (S41):** cost out each delivery level, then "five or ten X that cost" (S41 04:38), 5x as
  the floor because estimates run over; more for richer buyers (the "zip code tax").
- **Tiers (S31):** each tier 5-10x the one below with about 20% take-up, and each tier should add another full amount of
  revenue or it is not worth the complexity. "do not underestimate the power of large prices in small quantities" (S31 23:40).
  Customers are fractal: 1% can pay as much as the other 99% (S36 33:18).
- **Never discount, change terms (S15, S17):** "Because I, I never discount. But I will change terms" (S17 27:23). A lower
  price must come with less (no guarantee, fewer sessions). Prepay discounts are a term, not a discount (see 7).
- **Score:** where does the option sit on the ladder? Raising price with a close rate above 40%: 8-9. Holding price at 30-40%
  with educated leads: 6. Cutting price with a close rate above 30%: 1-2. Cutting price below 30% without fixing the sales
  process first: 2.

## 5. LTGP to CAC, the core arbitrage (B2, "Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff"; S08, S28)

- **What:** lifetime gross profit (not revenue) per customer against the full cost to acquire one (ads, sales pay,
  software). B2 rule of thumb: "Every business I invest in that struggles to scale has at least one thing in common -
  their LTGP to CAC ratio was less than 3 to 1" (B2). Estimate LTGP from last year's gross profit / customers, which errs
  low (S08). Spend at most a third of LTGP to get a customer (B2 Employees chapter, S08).
- **Humans in the loop (S28):** the more people in attraction, conversion and delivery, the more padding you need, because
  each new rep or tech is worse than the first: 0 humans 3:1, 1 human 6:1, 2 humans (a typical service business) 9:1,
  3 humans 12:1.
- **Which lever to pull (B2):** CAC between competitors is closer than you think; winners make more per customer. If your
  CAC is within 3x the industry average, work on LTGP (offer, price, upsells, retention); above 3x, work on ads or sales.
  "leads costing too much is rarely ever an issue. The issue is usually that you don't make enough money per customer" (S12 26:34).
- **Big ratios are a window:** Gym Launch's first year ran 100:1 ($100K spent, $10M made) (S28 12:04). When a window opens,
  back the truck up; he cares about absolute return, not ratio (S08 29:58).
- **Score:** ratio vs the humans-in-the-loop bar. At or above bar: 7-8; 2x the bar: 9-10; between 1x and the bar: 4-5;
  below 1:1: 1-2. An option that raises LTGP at flat CAC (an upsell, a price rise, a retention fix) gains 2 points.

## 6. Client-financed acquisition and Money Models (B2; $100M Money Models via S11, S16, S17, S18, S19)

- **The floor (B2):** if a customer pays back the cost to get and serve them inside 30 days, cash never limits growth.
  "I pick thirty days because any business can get interest free money for thirty days in the form of a credit card" (B2).
  B2's example: $15/month membership, $10 gross profit, $30 CAC; a $100 upsell taken by 1 in 5 adds $20, so the first 30
  days cover CAC. His claim: every company since 2017 crossed $1M/month in 12 months this way, without outside money.
- **The Money Models target (2025):** "Gross profit in thirty days is, uh, is greater than, uh, two times CAC plus COGS"
  (S17 01:49), so one customer pays for himself and the next one (S16 94:27, S18 138:27; S11 20:05 states it as 2 x CAC +
  COGS). "if you can do that in the first month, then it removes money as a constraint to growth" (S19 05:07).
- **A money model:** "And so a money model is a deliberate sequence of offers" (S17 01:12). Four objectives and their
  offer types (S16, S19): get them to buy (attraction offers: win your money back, giveaways, buy X get Y free); spend more
  (upsells: classic "you can't have X without Y", menu upsell, anchor, rollover); spend faster (pull cash forward:
  prepay, quarterly billing, setup fees); buy again (continuity: recurring or reoccurring, waived-fee commitments).
- **Diagnose before adding:** demand problem means attraction offer; LTV problem means upsell; conversion problem means
  downsell; repeat problem means continuity (S17, S19 31:35). Five moments to sell: immediately, after activation, halfway,
  last chance, at a milestone (S17). "You wanna sell at the point of greatest deprivation, not the point of greatest
  value" (S17 11:26). Asking only at the end gets about a third; asking mid-process gets half to two-thirds (S39 11:47).
- **Downsells and anchors (S17):** first downsell drops value a lot and price a little, to re-upsell the core ("$2,000 less,
  but no guarantee"); the second drops price more. Anchor very high: if 10% buy at 10x, revenue doubles (S17 23:41).
- **His gym model (S17, S19):** $10 lead, $500 challenge up front, $200 supplements at about 80% margin 48 hours later,
  rollover to membership at week 3, annual prepay offer: about $1,000 up front when rivals collect $99. "it cost me fifty
  dollars to make a thousand" (S17 05:48).
- **The brand exception:** "when you have a brand, basically all the rules that I write about don't matter" (S19 32:19).
- **Score:** 30-day gross profit / (CAC + COGS). Below 1: 1-3 (needs outside cash; fix the model before spending). 1 to 2:
  5-7. Above 2: 8-10. "Like, I will not hit the gas until I have a way" (S19 26:15).

## 7. Pull cash forward (S11, S28, S41, S43, S17)

- **What:** get paid sooner; it is often the first fix for cash flow. "If you wanna make more money, ask to get paid
  sooner" (S11 22:52). "Can we change payment terms? Which is the first thing you're gonna do if you have cash flow
  issues" (S17 31:49).
- **Rules of thumb (S28):** give at least 10% off for paid in full; buy-10-get-2 gets 15-20% take-up; a moderate discount
  plus speed or ease bonuses gets 30-40%; payment order: prepay, then half now half in 30 days, then three payments. 10-20%
  off for prepaying a year of events is worth it because small businesses earn more on capital than the stock market
  (S41 09:03). Card on file before you ask for money (S43 18:21). The first payment drives conversion more than the total
  (S30 52:33). Financing grows a business but never saves one: "If your food sucks at your restaurant, financing it will
  just get more people to find out that the food sucks faster" (S28 51:10).
- **Score:** days until cash covers the cost of the sale. Under 7 days: 9-10. Under 30: 7-8. 30-90: 4-5. Paid after
  delivery on net 60: 2.

## 8. Retention: the resale business (S11, S28, S29, S34, S38)

- **What:** "If you do not have what's called revenue retention, you have nothing" (S34 00:35). Company A sells 100, 200,
  300 a year and loses them; Company B sells 100 a year and keeps them; same revenue, but A must buy twice the customers
  and the marginal ones cost 2-3x more (S34, S38). LTV is roughly annual revenue / churn: 50% retention gives about 2x a
  year's revenue, 80% gives about 5x (S28). B2B target: 80%+ annual retention on the original cohort (S28). SaaS-like
  stickiness: revenue retention over 100% through upgrades (S29, S34).
- **Where churn lives:** month one is the worst (Skool data: over 20% in month one, about 10% by month three, about 2% a
  month after six), so the first 30 days decide LTV (S34 02:52). Customers judge you in the first 24 hours after purchase
  (S16 49:53). "The reason someone buys isn't always necessarily the reason someone stays" (S30 41:17).
- **Agency lens:** a client who stays 2+ years is permanent; if 1 in 10 become permanent, true CAC is 10x CAC (S11 36:56).
  Small-business clients churn even when the work is good (S36 43:29).
- **Score:** does the option raise month-one activation, renewal, or ascension? +3 for each. An option that buys more
  leads into a leaking base scores 3 at best.

## 9. Core Four, lead getters, Rule of 100, More Better New (B2, Section III-V)

- **Definitions (B2):** a lead is a person you can contact; an engaged lead has shown interest. "Engaged leads are the
  true output of advertising" (B2). Lead magnets are mini offers: "a complete solution to a narrow problem" that reveals
  the next problem (S25 01:58).
- **Core four:** warm outreach, posting free content, cold outreach, paid ads (known vs unknown people, one-to-one vs
  one-to-many). Then four lead getters who do the core four for you: customers (referrals), employees, agencies,
  affiliates. "Those are the only four things that one person can do" (S16 43:31).
- **Rule of 100 (B2, "More Better New"):** "You advertise your stuff by doing 100 primary actions every day, for one
  hundred days in a row" (B2): 100 reach-outs, or 100 minutes of content, or 100 minutes making ads.
- **More, Better, New (B2):** do more until something breaks; then fix the step with the biggest drop-off (one test per
  platform per week; four failed tries means move to the next constraint); only then go new, in the order new placement,
  new platform, new core four activity. On camera: "More is typically the highest risk-adjusted return strategy" (S13 24:25);
  "the cost of change of doing something new is guaranteed, but the return on something new is not" (S08 13:49). One closer
  at 30% pushed to 40% is a 33% lift with risk; three more closers is a 300% lift (S13 25:20). He allows himself one new
  thing a year (S27 48:54).
- **Paid ads money rules (B2):** three phases: track money, lose money, print money. Test budget: "I budget two times the
  cash I collect from a customer in thirty days (not LTGP) when testing new ads" (B2). Once profitable, budget backwards
  from how many customers you can handle, plus 20% padding.
- **Volume:** "what presents as volatility is typically a symptom of low volume" (S13 29:15). Companies doing 30x your
  sales do about 30x your advertising (S28 19:59). Under about $1-3M, spend the first four hours of every day on one core
  four activity (S13 00:48, S08).
- **Score:** is the option "more" of a working channel (8), "better" on the constraint step (7), or "new" while a working
  channel is under-run (2-3)?

## 10. One constraint at a time: supply or demand, back to front (S07, S08, S37, S43, S38, S41)

- **What:** first question: "From a time perspective, does it take longer to sell or does it take longer to deliver?"
  (S43 03:24); or "if you double the amount of customers you have, does it break anything?" (S37 06:20). Supply
  constrained means fix capacity (raise price, change the delivery ratio, productise, hire) (S30 16:32); demand constrained
  means leads, offer and sales. "uh, more marketing on a supply constraint does nothing" (S43 31:37). He estimates about 70%
  of $1-50M businesses that come to him are demand constrained (S38 93:19).
- **The 10x question:** "What would stop me from ten X-ing the business right now?" (S08 13:02). Fix the first thing that
  breaks, then add volume. "the business as a whole will only be as strong as its weakest link" (S07 13:54). Founders keep
  fixing the problem they enjoy: a 40% closer asking about sales scripts does not have a sales constraint (S07).
- **Order of repair:** "if you think about fixing a business, I always fix things from back to front" (S35 05:09): offer,
  then price, then sales motion, then demand (S41 04:03). "we don't wanna jam more demand through something that's thin
  margins" (S41 32:17). His 2025 flip: when supply and demand are both maxed, add supply first; demand first costs
  reputation, supply first costs profit (S27 72:28).
- **Score:** does the option touch the current constraint? Yes and it relieves it: 8-10. Touches a non-constraint: 3-4,
  however good it is in the abstract. Adds demand onto a supply constraint or thin margins: 1-2.

## 11. Business shape: margin, the five advantages, and the multiple (S28, S29, S34, S24)

- **Gross margin floor:** "I will not get into a business with less than eighty percent gross margins" (S28 39:09), for
  services. On a $100 cost, 70% margin means a $333 price, 80% means $500, 90% means $1,000 (S28). A $100M business at 10%
  margin makes the same as a $20M one at 50% (S34 08:07).
- **Five advantages (S34):** sticky (the most important), expensive (gross margin), expansion (a growing market), "air"
  (low operational complexity and capex), unique (moat). All continuums. High return on capital makes you a magnet for
  money; if you can't raise, the economics are the problem, not the pitch (S34 14:40).
- **Four shapes (S29):** e-commerce (capped by free cash flow), service (talent-bound, slow and steady, "the least risky
  business because you can always keep a service business profitable", S29 23:52), education (fast then plateau at $1-3M),
  SaaS (slow, then the most scalable). In service, "what they charge" is his single progress metric (S29 33:13), and he would
  rather make $1M revenue per person (S29 35:29).
- **Owner-independence and the multiple (B2 Employees chapter; S24):** "You get rich from what you make. You become wealthy
  from what you own" (B2). Two $10M businesses with $2M profit: the one that runs without the owner sells at about 6x; the
  one that needs him is a job. "a business that requires you isn't a business" (S24 00:46).
- **Score:** gross margin band (below 50%: 2; 50-70%: 5; 80%+: 8), +1 for stickiness, +1 if the option makes the business
  less dependent on the founder.

## 12. People as an investment with a return (B2 Employees chapter; S07, S14, S26, S27, S32)

- **Leverage maths (B2):** trade 40 hours of doing for 4 of managing; 200 hours of doing becomes 20 of managing, then a
  manager costs you 4. Train with the 3Ds: document, demonstrate, duplicate. Measure lead-getting staff as payroll / engaged
  leads = cost per lead, then CAC against LTGP. "Don't fire your sales guy if you've got advertising problems" (B2).
- **Talent ROI:** "I think the highest returns on capital we get as entrepreneurs is talent. Like full stop" (S26 26:18).
  His returns grew with pay: a $50K manager returned 5x, a $300K salesperson 14x, a $1M executive 50x (S07 82:12). Rep math
  in a service business: about $2M revenue and $1M gross profit per rep on $200K pay, 5:1, plus enterprise value (S32 40:56).
  "I have yet to make less money by hiring more salespeople" (S28 28:26), as long as there is lead flow (S44 27:50).
- **The swamp ($1-3M):** at $1M revenue and 20% margin an A player can cost about 100% of profit; both paths (hire now, or
  work six more hours a day until $3-4M) work, and it is an impossible choice between two bad options (S14 123:17, 124:13).
  Size a hire by the founder time it frees: the role that eats about 40% of your week (S14 133:42).
- **Sales capacity check:** rep calendars at 70-75% booked, never above 85% or below 60%; above, add reps (S28).
- **Score:** expected gross profit the hire produces or frees in year one / fully loaded cost. Above 5x: 9; 3-5x: 7; 1-3x:
  5; below 1x or no defined output: 2. "How does what you do make the business money?" (S07 19:56) is the gate question.

## 13. AI: workflows, not roles; money, not activity (S33, S36, S38, S32)

- **What:** "you have to stop thinking in role-based thinking and start thinking in workflow-based thinking" (S33 04:29).
  List the 4-10 tasks a role does and ask which could live in a workflow instead of headcount. Train AI like a new hire:
  define what good looks like, give rules plus samples, iterate. AI-first needs data-first (S32 24:26); a data layer is what
  small businesses miss (S36 27:50).
- **The money test:** "Just the very simple: Are you making more money now?" (S38 03:33). The case he uses: a business spent
  $350,000 building AI to replace 11 VAs costing about $11,000 a month, on a process that was not the constraint; "a process
  they could automate, not that they should automate it" (S38 04:20).
- **Price lag:** buyers' price sensitivity adapts slowly, so keep human prices while costs fall; margins balloon for a while
  (S33 21:42, S36 24:55). Don't advertise "AI"; advertise the outcome (S38).
- **Score:** start from framework 10. If the automated workflow is the constraint or frees founder or delivery capacity that
  is: 7-9. If it saves cost on a non-constraint with a payback over 12 months: 3-4. AI used to "do dumb things really fast"
  (S36 23:33): 1.

## 14. Bets: downside first, fewest need-to-believes (S14, S20, S23, S36, S44, S45, B1 opening)

- **What:** "If you can't live with the downside, don't take the bet" (S36 51:07). Plans with the fewest assumptions win:
  "every single new assumption that we introduce, like, dramatically decrease the likely that it occurs" (S20 23:06). Ask how
  wrong you can be and still be right (S23 106:59). Size bets to the bankroll: "most entrepreneurs go in with only enough
  money to d- to play two hands" (S44 25:11); but "if you have a ten to one play that you put every dollar you put in, you
  get ten back, you should run that play a whole lot of times" (S44 26:50). Quitting a job: guarantee bad, chance at good
  (S14 14:26). B1 opens with Bezos on a 10% chance at a 100x payoff.
- **Pivot or push:** pivot only if a founding assumption was proven untrue by your own activity; push if it is only slower or
  harder than hoped (S38 64:58, S16 90:16).
- **Score:** start at 5. +2 if the downside is survivable and capped, +2 if it rests on one need-to-believe, +1 if it can be
  tested small first ($1,000 per market, S14 148:19). -3 if it bets the payroll on more than two assumptions.
