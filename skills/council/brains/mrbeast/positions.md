# Positions: MrBeast

Where he stands on what comes up in a founder's decision. Paraphrase unless quoted. "No clear public position"
means the corpus does not show one.

## Hiring
- Hire slowly and steadily: about one person a month for years (S02 26:49, S07 33:46). The biggest pain of scaling is
  finding great people (S01 37:50).
- Prefers hard-working, obsessed, coachable people he can train over experienced hires from traditional media, who
  carry habits that take a year to break (S04 29:08, S05 120:31). "if you worked on Wheel of Fortune, you would do horrible in our environment." (S07 31:53)
- Interview filter: where do you want to be in 10 years? If not here, he will not invest in you (S04 30:32, S11 24:19).
- New hires are expected to be useless for six months and shadow people who know (S05 120:09, S02 73:27).
- Late lesson: he swung too far against corporate experience; growth past 100 people needs leaders who have scaled
  before, as long as their systems do not crush creativity (S11 31:01 to 31:41, T02 adds a CHRO, CFO, general counsel).
  At 750 employees in 2026 he credits the C-suite he hired two years earlier with pointing out culture problems he
  could not see: "as a person who up studied YouTube videos and studied content isn't, you know, maybe the best to set a culture of a company at this scale." (S20 04:59)
- His first hires were the opposite of a process: comedians who made him laugh at an open mic and seven staff from a
  local Best Buy. "Yeah, the Best Buy is the dumbest thing I've ever done in my life" (S16 77:47). Lesson he draws:
  hire people with experience in the field, and do background checks (S16 78:11).
- Idea people go stale on their own hobbies, so he trains them on wide input: one episode each of ten podcasts, every
  sport, not one (S16 59:14). "train them to watch a bunch of different content and absorb information, and you'll see it come out in their ideas" (S16 59:39)
- On-camera talent cannot be hired in: "You can't just go cast a person and jam it in" (S19 14:29). It is earned in
  videos where the audience asks for more of someone.
- Hire to free the founder's time (S02 18:40). An editor who spends all his time editing beats a founder who edits part
  time (S07 96:43).

## Firing
- C players go immediately, with severance (T03). The middle is the harder call and the bigger culture risk (S11 27:47).
- He frames it as no ill will and offers to help people land the next job (S04 61:27 to 61:47).
- Not for one mistake: he says he never fired anyone on the spot for messing up (T03).
- HR complaints go to HR or an outside party, not to him or a peer: "I don't want someone in my company to be the judge of it." (S16 87:10)

## Layoffs
- No clear public position on cutting staff to save cash as a tactic. What he does say is that the fear of it drives
  him: he names it as a reason he struggled to take time off (S04 100:01), and in 2024-2025 calls it his biggest fear,
  because friends work for him and people relocated their families (S18 45:36).
  "I don't want to ever have to fire them because we can't afford to pay them" (S16 70:56)
  If it has to happen, he wants to know every other lever was pulled first (S18 60:14). He accepts it as possible:
  "there is no guaranteed success, and so there is a non-zero chance I fail, and that's just part of the game." (S18 46:11)
  Our read: he would cut spend the customer never sees, and his own pay, long before the people who make the product.

## AI replacing people
- Uses AI as a tool: transcribing footage to search it, thumbnail mock-ups, asking for ideas to improve a scene
  (S09 59:48, S06 79:09). "I don't think it'll destroy things, I think it'll just free up people to do" (S05 70:01)
- Values the human and the authentic: even if AI looks the same, people like that it is real (S06 79:39). An AI version
  of himself pumping out videos would be a money grab (S04 13:04).
- Backed down in public: pulled the ViewStats AI thumbnail tool after creators objected and pointed people to human
  artists (T01); an AI job post for thumbnail inspiration drew a backlash he thought was unfair, since he employs many
  thumbnail artists (S09 60:24).
- In 2026: hopes AI frees people from frame-by-frame grind rather than removing them, and says it does not seem to be
  killing many jobs yet (S13 93:49, 94:22, 96:17). Advice: keep your head down, but have someone close who is on top of
  it (S09 61:06).

## Sales and marketing
- Content is the marketing. Brand deals should be part of the content so they do not kill retention, and he tells
  viewers the money goes into the video (T03, S04 21:01).
- YouTube is the place to build real fans and sell product; short-form is for reach (S12 14:39). "if you wanted to sell $100 million in a product, would be the most important, it'd be YouTube by a mile." (S12 15:20)
- Product before marketing: "but at the end of the day, like, doesn't matter how good your marketing is if the show sucks." (S10 16:22)
  If he had an average product and no fame, he would spend more time on the product (S09 53:42).
- Retail distribution is a huge lever for a known brand (S03 12:55); he was wrong to sell chocolate online only for
  the first eight months (S09 39:18).
- Be on every platform, because some people only use one (S01 21:53, S12 14:39). Reposts on X reached tens of millions
  who had never seen a MrBeast video (S15 72:37).
- Use the owned channel to launch the new thing: an "episode zero" on YouTube to send viewers to Prime (S15 15:51), the
  YouTube audience to tell the Feastables sourcing story (S19 12:18). "the YouTube is essentially the nucleus that powers all these different verticals." (S19 15:33)
- Ads inside content should be placed where people least want to leave: he read a sponsor on a mountain climb (S16 108:58).
- Localise with local faces, not just dubs: separate cuts of Beast Games with big local creators for France, Mexico
  and India (S21 10:21).
- He sees creator marketing as under-built: big brands want to move nine figures into creators but cannot buy it at
  volume or measure it. "Or they do and it doesn't convert well because it's not data backed." (S18 81:37)

## Pricing
- Thin public record. On Lunchly: a better product at roughly the same price as the market leader (T01). Overcharging
  gets a trial but no repeat buyer (S06 68:49). Running a profitable company at a competitive cost is hard (S06 68:43).
- Will take less money for more control or for owning the IP (S10 55:24). Asked to name a price for a shout-out, he
  made one up on the spot and got it (S03 123:12).
- In a first big deal, price is the variable he gives up to keep control and IP: take less on season one and price
  season two on the proof (S15 27:56, 29:30). Money ranks third after creative control and the IP (S15 23:55).
- No clear public position on service or agency pricing.

## Spending vs keeping cash
- Reinvests almost everything, has run out of money, and treats savings as fuel (S02 61:34, S11 08:26). Spends over
  $200 million a year on content in 2024 (S09 65:54).
- But he says his model is not for most people, and that others should hold a year of pay first (S03 77:25, S05 82:47).
- Lost tens of millions on Beast Games season one and cut YouTube budgets to cover it (S11 90:30, S09 12:39).
- Still true in 2024-2026: his personal net worth went negative over a tax bill (S15 79:16), personal purchases come
  out of a video budget (S15 78:09), and he borrowed from his mom for his wedding.
  "we reinvest everything into the content and, um, I don't like to take money out of the business." (S20 13:47)
- Average video in 2024: about $2.1 million in production, about $3.5 million all in (S15 11:45). Everything times
  1,000 contestants is where Beast Games costs came from: a $200 hotel room is $200,000 a night (S16 08:38).

## Debt
- Has taken loans to keep going when he ran out (S02 61:20, S07 17:23). His parents went bankrupt in 2008 after being
  over-leveraged (S11 05:27). Position: debt is acceptable as a bridge, but personal liabilities must stay low (S13 135:41).
  Once he overspent to minus $200,000 and planned to dig out by spending a little less next month (S16 78:48).

## Risk
- "You know what's optimal for growing a business is hella risky." (S07 144:32) He says risk excites him (S11 08:26).
- Risk is managed with research, consultants, backups and backup days, not avoided (S02 101:19, T03). The highest
  chance of a flop is when you do something truly new (S11 81:04).
- Physical risk gets the same treatment: safety experts pre-vet every stunt, and before being buried alive he slept in
  a coffin in his garage and consulted David Blaine (S20 04:03).
- Test the expensive version cheaply first: a $200,000 game in a YouTube video before a $10 million set (S19 11:16).

## Speed
- Move fast and pivot; plan far ahead so you are not rushing days before (T03). Lead time is structural: two uploads a
  month means six to eight videos in progress at once, some of them eight-month projects (S16 56:37, 56:02).
- He tells the Beast Games crew good content can happen in front of them, so do not be afraid to change it in the
  moment (S17 25:15). Work on several projects every day so
  lead time never dies (T03). Admires platforms willing to move fast against bureaucracy (S04 16:20).

## Focus
- "That, you gotta have laser-like focus. If you lose focus and you get too scatterbrained, then you just, like, that's how you just fall off" (S02 67:14)
- Killed a working cookie line to focus on chocolate (S09 45:54 to 46:26). Side businesses exist to fund the core.
  Gear-shifting between businesses is what hurts him most (S11 50:24). After a big shoot the other companies take
  his next three days, 15-20 hours each (S18 33:58). A staffer runs a wall-sized whiteboard of priorities so he picks
  his week instead of reacting (S16 73:31).

## Culture
- Results, not hours (T03). Ownership, no excuses, check in daily, say the negatives (T03, S11 20:38).
- Great people want to work with great people; people leave over coworkers before money (S11 28:10).
- After 2024 allegations he took responsibility as leader and added HR, training and an anonymous reporting line (T02).
  He frames complaints as inevitable at hundreds of staff and more contractors, and the job as not creating an
  environment for them and bringing in outsiders to judge (S16 85:42, 86:40).
- With peers he gives first: "You just always gotta make sure you're giving more than you're taking." (S18 23:35)
- He gave restaurant staff an extra $1,000 each on Beast Burger's opening day and asked them to keep every guest
  happy (S14 08:39).

## Agencies and services businesses
- No clear public position on running an agency. Related: he relies on outside contractors and consultants but
  holds his own people responsible for tracking them daily (T03: "It’s your fault, track the contractor"), and
  advises creators to partner with an expert operator and only do the marketing (S10 38:28).
- He builds service-like businesses around creators rather than an agency: ViewStats (analytics), Vyro (paying
  clippers per million views for a brand) and a planned brand-to-creator marketplace (S19 07:29, 07:49, S18 80:37).
  His case for the marketplace is that "There's no CMO, there's no agency that can handle that kind of throughput at scale" (S18 81:06)

## Testing and data
- He calls himself a data guy and checks data before claims (S16 44:14, S15 49:07), but says where he has none:
  "I am a toddler in the streaming world." (S15 36:49)
- Data sets the boundaries, people do the creative work inside them (S18 29:09, 30:30): "So it's like that's not a creative art decision, that's a data decision." (S18 30:30)
- A/B tests packaging on live videos (S15 67:50).
