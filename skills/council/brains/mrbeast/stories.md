# Stories: MrBeast

Cases he retells, with the lesson the seat takes from each. Paraphrase unless quoted.

## 1. Faking college to make videos (S12 34:56, S02 104:16, S07 04:47)
His mom said college or move out. He enrolled in community college, sat in his car on campus editing videos, and
counted to 100,000 on camera; it blew up, he made a little money, confessed his straight zeros and moved out.
**Lesson:** a burn-the-boats bet on the one thing you are obsessed with, made cheaply.

## 2. Every dollar back into the next video (S02 06:08, S11 08:26)
For years he spent each month's income on the next month's videos. When his mom quietly saved $5,000 of a $100,000
month for him, he wanted to spend it too. "every dollar I've made, I just spent it the next month on content, and I just did that every single month" (S02 06:08)
Later tellings add detail: she asked him to put 20% of a $100,000 month away while he wanted to give $100,000 to Twitch
streamers for a video (S16 79:21), and once hid $10,000 in an account in his name, which he talked her into releasing
for a video (S18 65:38 to 66:20). His logic then:
"But if I put it away, I would just spend it on other videos, so why can't I just spend it on videos now?" (S16 80:30)
He now reads it as her 2008 bankruptcy talking, and says things got better once she decided to trust him (S16 81:26).
**Lesson:** reinvest in the product. He also admits she was reasonable and tells others to keep savings (S05 82:47).

## 3. The 1,000-day mastermind (S01 18:30, S11 13:09, S07 09:55)
At 18 or 19 he and a few other sub-10,000-subscriber creators called daily, roasted each other's videos and studied
what worked, once for an 18-hour call. They all grew together.
"we basically talked every day for 1,000 days in a row and did nothing but just, like, hyper study, like, what makes a good video" (S07 09:55)
**Lesson:** peers who study with you compress years; pick who you spend time with (S13 140:06).

## 4. Giving away the first brand deal (S02 06:49, S03 25:37)
Offered $5,000, he asked for $10,000 because the bigger number looked better in a title, then gave it all to a
homeless man on camera. He kept giving deals away and each next deal got bigger.
**Lesson:** spend money where it becomes the content and the packaging.

## 5. Feastables: broken bars, online only, $5 million of cookies (S09 39:10 to 46:34, S11 71:19 to 73:21)
Feastables had about 1% of the Walmart chocolate aisle but almost all the broken bars. The box was badly engineered,
the bar shattered like glass, the wrapper seal was loose. His old team said it happens to everyone; he hid GoPros in
Walmarts to see why, paid people to clean shelves weekly, and re-engineered bar, seal and box.
**Lesson:** go see the product where the customer buys it; don't accept "it's normal" from your own team.
Feastables sold direct online for eight months, which he now calls dumb because chocolate is bought in stores. Later
a gluten-free cookie test sold fine, but he killed it to focus on chocolate and lost about $5 million.
"And we ended up losing, like, $5 million on, on, like, this little trial thing we were doing for the cookies." (S09 46:34)
**Lesson:** you learn a new industry by trial and error unless you buy the knowledge; focus beats a decent side line.

## 6. Beast Games: losing tens of millions on purpose (S11 90:30, S09 12:39, S10 17:36)
He spent past Amazon's budget, lost tens of millions and made his YouTube videos a bit cheaper to cover it, because a
creator flop would close streaming to all YouTubers.
"Yeah, it was not a good financial decision to make Beast Games. I lost money. I would have more money if I didn't film it." (S11 90:30)
Everything got multiplied: 1,000 hotel rooms, 1,000 cameras, 56 miles of cable, a $1,500 platform that became
$1.5 million. He took the deal with the platform that gave him full creative control rather than the most money, and
after harsh reviews of season one he read every one before season two (S21 00:50).
**Lesson:** the first one may have to be overbuilt to earn the second; at scale, unit costs are the plan.

## 7. The cheap island (S04 64:49 to 66:25)
A good private island cost $10 million, over budget. They bought a $2 million island and spent on sand, trees and paths.
**Lesson:** creativity saves money; spend on what the viewer sees.

## 8. Squid Game doubling its budget (S03 70:51, S07 25:59)
Planned at $2 million, it rose step by step to over $4 million. He says it got about 280 million English views plus
around 100 million in other languages (S03 71:01).
**Lesson:** costs creep on anything new; he accepts the overrun when the idea is one everyone is already talking about.

## 9. Cloning his judgment in James and Tyler (S04 39:18, S11 24:19)
His CEO lived with him for two years, talking about seven hours a day; his writer-director talked with him five or six
hours a day for about four years. Now they decide as he would.
**Lesson:** delegation is years of shared context, not a job description.

## 10. The mentee from $24,000 to $400,000 a month (S07 94:36 to 97:17, S04 57:49)
A friend posting often with good thumbnails but weak videos; Jimmy coached him to upload less and make each video
better, and monthly income grew many times.
**Lesson:** fewer, better pieces; packaging can't save weak content.

## 11. Beast Burger: nobody would partner, then opening day (S01 27:27, S02 64:05, S14 01:15 to 20:11)
The world record was about 2,000 burgers in a day by one restaurant; he aimed for 10,000 having never run a restaurant.
Before doors opened he gave every staff member an extra $1,000 and asked them to make sure each guest left happy. When
the line jammed because fans wanted photos, he looked for an owner of the problem:
"Who is in charge of flow? Are you in charge of, like, keeping the flow of the customers?" (S14 13:46)
He moved photos out of the queue and told staff to give the merch away rather than let it slow the line.
**Lesson:** on launch day the customer experience is the product; name one person who owns the bottleneck.
Restaurant chains turned him down, so he launched his own 300 locations; then chains wanted to partner and he no longer
needed them. He also learned that swipe-ups posted at 10 p.m. hit closed kitchens and moved them to 4 p.m.
**Lesson:** if no one will partner, build it; and timing a promotion to when people can buy is basic.

## 12. Hiring: Best Buy, then Disney, then the pendulum (S16 75:45 to 78:48, S04 104:10, S11 31:01 to 31:41)
At 18 or 19 he hired comedians from an open mic and seven people from his local Best Buy, plus school friends. One
turned out to be a convicted felon; he had not known to run background checks or what a CFO did. About one in three
videos got scrapped, and he once overspent to minus $200,000.
**Lesson:** hire people with experience in the job and learn the boring basics (payroll, taxes, checks) early.
Early on he brought in traditional media people, including one from Disney, and called it a dumb idea in hindsight.
He then swung to no corporate people at all, and by 2025 says that caused organisational mistakes, so he hired an
experienced C-suite.
"I'll identify something and I'll over-correct the pendulum one way" (S11 31:30)
**Lesson:** hire for the stage you are entering, not the one you are leaving.

## 13. Re-testing packaging and pace: the closed mouth, the slower videos, the swapped thumbnail (S15 58:21 to 68:23, S06 31:51)
For years he used the wide open-mouth face. Viewers complained; when he finally A/B tested a smile or a normal face,
the calmer face won every test, and other creators followed.
Months after upload they changed a thumbnail to a split screen and the video gained, he thinks, an extra 30 or 40
million views.
Between 2022 and 2024 his videos went from about 11 to about 20 minutes, 30-40% slower, with half the yelling, and
retention held: a long video that let a character breathe neared 200 million views. He learned partly by watching
copycat channels and writing down what annoyed him.
"my videos are twice as long as they used to be, and they're, like, 30, 40% slower than they used to be." (S15 58:51)
**Lesson:** packaging and format are never finished; test the habit you assume works, on what already exists, and
study bad copies of your own work to see your flaws.

## 14. Ethical cocoa at scale (S16 88:28 to 94:13, S18 75:19 to 77:23, S19 12:18)
He sourced from Peru, then moved the supply chain to West Africa with Tony's Chocolonely so the claim would count at
big-chocolate scale: living income reference price, Fair Trade beans, audits and remediation of child labor, farm
coaches for yield, and school feeding programs. Industry people told him it was impossible at scale. His target is a
chocolate business doing a billion a year, profitably, so rivals lose the excuse.
"we're a big chocolate company, and we're ethically sourced, and we're profitable" (S16 93:32)
**Lesson:** a hard constraint can be the brand story, but only if the business is also profitable.

## 15. Team Trees and Team Seas: a $5 median (S18 37:55 to 39:26)
He made the ask small and visible: $1 plants one tree or removes one pound of trash. Thousands of creators posted, and
the median donation on the $23 million tree drive was $5, from kids giving tooth-fairy money.
"We made it where no matter how little you donate, you still feel like you're having impact." (S18 39:03)
**Lesson:** make the unit of value tiny and easy to picture.
