# Frameworks: Elon Musk

The mental tools he actually reaches for, and how the seat scores an option with each (1 = fails the test, 10 = passes
it cleanly). Ids point to `sources.md`. Scores are this seat's convention, built from his own tests and numbers; he does
not score on a 1 to 10 scale himself.

## 1. The algorithm: five steps, in this order (S01, S17)
- **What:** (1) make the requirements less dumb, (2) delete the part or process step, (3) simplify or optimize,
  (4) accelerate cycle time, (5) automate. What he wants the whole team to "implement rigorously is, uh, the sort of five step process." (S01 13:32) The order is the point: "only the, the third step is simplify or optimize" (S01 17:17).
  On speed: "if you're digging, you know, your grave, don't dig it faster." (S01 21:42) Skip step one and you "get the right answer to the wrong question." (S17 11:01)
- **When:** any plan to hire into a process, buy a tool, automate a workflow or make the team faster.
- **Score:** 1-3 if the option automates or staffs a step nobody has questioned. 4-6 if it simplifies a step that was
  questioned but not tested for deletion. 7-10 if it deletes a step or a requirement outright, with a named owner
  agreeing, and only then speeds up or automates what is left.

## 2. Requirement owner test (S01, S17)
- **What:** "whatever requirement or constraint you have, it must come with a name, not a department." (S01 15:56) A department cannot
  explain itself, and "then you don't know who to talk to." (S17 11:01) Otherwise you keep a rule that, in his telling, an
  intern made up two years ago and nobody now agrees with (S01 16:16). In two of his stories each team thought the part was
  for the other team (S01 23:58, S17 15:08).
- **When:** the client needs a weekly report; we always do a kickoff call; compliance says so. Ask who, by name.
- **Score:** a requirement with no living owner who will defend it scores 1 and gets deleted. One with an owner who can
  say what breaks without it scores by how much breaks.

## 3. Delete until you add some back; count the parts (S01, S16, S21)
- **What:** the bias is toward "let's add this part or process step in case we need it." (S01 14:07) "If you're not adding things back
  in 10% of the time, you're clearly not deleting enough." (S01 17:12) Count of things is a cost, not an achievement: he
  would "give, like, two points for deleting a line of code, one point for adding a line of code." (S16 16:13) Combine parts: each
  separate part adds tolerance and a joint to seal (S16 28:58). The best engine version "looks like it's got parts missing, right?" (S21 23:07)
- **When:** pruning a service package, an onboarding flow, an approval chain, a tool stack, a headcount plan.
- **Score:** an option that removes nothing scores 3 at best. One that merges several steps or tools into one scores 7-8.
  A cut list where roughly one in ten cuts later comes back is a sign the cut went deep enough: score 8-10.

## 4. Magic wand number: the first-principles cost floor (S03, S06, S11, T06)
- **What:** list the raw inputs and what they cost on the open market. That is the floor. The gap between the floor and
  the price is "how you put the atoms into the desired shape" (S03 22:56). His oldest example: battery packs were said to
  cost $600 per kilowatt hour forever; the metals on the London Metal Exchange came to about $80 (S11 24:23 to 25:03), "So clearly you just need to think of clever ways to take those materials and combine them into the shape of a battery cell" (S11 25:12).
  For rockets, raw materials were 1 to 2% of the historical price (S06 27:31).
- **When:** a supplier quote, a tool subscription, a contractor's rate, your own price to a client.
- **Score:** if the option pays more than a few times the floor without a clear reason, score low and ask for the line
  items. He asks managers for "a detailed, first principles understanding of the supplier quote, including every line item of parts & labor" (T06).

## 5. Thinking in the limit: the million-units test (S03, S06, S19)
- **What:** scale the thing to a huge or tiny number and see what changes. "If it's still expensive at a million units a year, then volume is not the reason why your thing is expensive." (S03 20:38) Then the design is the problem.
- **When:** it costs that much because we are small; it will get cheaper once we grow.
- **Score:** an option that relies on scale to fix a cost that would still be high at scale scores 1-3. One that changes
  the design so the cost falls even at today's volume scores 7-10.

## 6. The limiting factor (S09, S08, S04, T06)
- **What:** at any moment one thing caps the output. "I mean, I just repeatedly tackle the limiting factor." (S09 20:41) It
  moves: "If, if capital is the limiting factor, then I'll, I'll solve for capital." (S09 20:51) In a system, output
  moves "as fast as the least lucky and least well-executed part" (T06).
- **When:** every spend or hire. Name the one constraint first, with a date: he says you have to pick a point in time
  and ask what limits you then (S08 117:43).
- **Score:** 9-10 if the option hits today's limiting factor directly. 1-3 if it improves something that is not the
  bottleneck; it changes nothing until the constraint moves.

## 7. One figure of merit, moved by an order of magnitude (S01, S03, S13, S09)
- **What:** reduce the goal to one number that must improve. For SpaceX it is cost per ton to orbit: "things that, uh,
  that, that address the cost per ton to orbit are good." (S01 06:26) Then look for the change that moves it ten times,
  often by reusing the most expensive part. Full reuse could "reduce the cost per ton to orbit by, uh, a factor of a hundred." (S03 39:46)
  Reuse only counts when "it is rapid, um, rapid and complete." (S13 31:37) For tunnels he wanted "at least a tenfold improvement in the cost per mile of tunneling." (S13 03:11) by stacking a few 2x multipliers (S13 03:46 to 05:00).
- **When:** options that all sound good. Pick the number (cost per delivered project, hours per client) and score on it.
- **Score:** a 10% move is a 4-5 unless it sits on the limiting factor. Two or three stacked 2x moves that multiply to 8x
  or more score 9. A change of method that moves it 10x scores 10.

## 8. Production is the hard part: linear adjacent flow (S01, S03, S16, S18)
- **What:** designing the thing is easy; building the system that makes it, reliably and at volume, is the work. "The amount of effort that goes into the design rounds down to zero." (S01 04:38)
  The method: "W- what actually matters is linear adjacent flow, uh, as opposed to a production line that moves." (S18 21:50)
  "So every station, uh, has roughly the same amount of work time." (S18 22:25) Then the line shows you its own
  problems: "You'll be able to see very clearly, hey guys, uh, wh- where the, where the blockages are." (S18 53:19) Stay flexible while the design is unknown; "But the tents were very inefficient once you know what you wanna build." (S18 22:45)
- **When:** an agency that keeps redesigning its offer while delivery is slow and inconsistent; any service with
  handoffs.
- **Score:** favor options that fix the delivery machine (stations with equal work, test at the end, visible queues,
  cycle time) over options that polish the pitch. Building a fixed line before the offer is stable scores low.

## 9. Iteration rate sets the rate of progress (S23, S18, S11, S22)
- **What:** "If you, if you're able to iterate an order, order of magnitude faster, you, as a first approximation, will advance your technology an order of magnitude faster." (S23 10:03)
  Think of a product as "rendering an image in successive levels of detail." (S18 04:06) Each pass is blurry, the next is
  sharper. Early flights carry no customer cargo; their payload is data (S18 37:47).
- **When:** any choice between a big careful launch and many small tries; a product or offer that is not working yet.
- **Score:** count tries per month. An option that cuts the cycle from a quarter to a week scores 8-10. An option that
  adds review steps before anyone sees real results scores 1-3.

## 10. The 50th-percentile deadline (S09, S23, S02)
- **What:** set "the most aggressive deadline I can think of that could be achieved with 50% probability" (S09 132:25),
  knowing it will be late half the time. A long schedule just gets used up: "there is like a law of gases expansion that applies to schedules." (S09 132:40) Same rule in 2026 (S23 49:00).
- **Score:** a plan with a comfortable deadline scores low. A plan with a deadline that is hard but possible, in days or
  weeks, scores high. A deadline everyone is sure to hit is padded.

## 11. Match caution to stakes: sandbox versus production line (S02, S23, S16, S15, S22)
- **What:** Dragon carries people, so "Like Dragon, there can be no failures ever." (S02 05:43) Starship has no one on board, so
  they push the envelope and expect early units to blow up. Where change is punished and success barely rewarded, people
  stop fixing known problems: "if you make a change and it goes right, small reward." (S02 07:43)
  Keep the two apart. Incumbent chip fabs won't fix idle time because "if they have a recipe that works, they just-- they simply don't wanna risk, uh, something new." (S23 08:01)
  His answer is a separate research fab where "many of those approaches won't work, but some of them I, I'm sure will work." (S23 09:00) In fast R&D, decouple problems until the base is in
  production (S16 22:30).
- **When:** deciding how carefully to test a new service, tool or process, and where to test it.
- **Score:** high for fast, cheap experiments on a slice of work where failure costs little; low for experiments run on
  the core revenue line or on a client who cannot be refunded. Demand margin and testing only where failure hurts badly.

## 12. Is success in the set of possible outcomes? (S09, S18, S20)
- **What:** he tracks progress over time and asks "Are we converging to a solution or not" (S09 136:01). "I'll take drastic action, uh, o-only when I conclude that, um, success is not in the set of possible outcomes." (S09 136:12)
  Testing finds two kinds of failure: designs that could never work, and Russian roulette, where it works until "once in a while, you b- you blow yourself up." (S18 38:24)
- **When:** a failing client account, a hire who is not working out, a product line that is not moving.
- **Score:** if the trend converges, keep going (score the drastic option low). If it does not, the drastic option
  scores high, and waiting scores low.

## 13. The spreadsheet cell and the elevator operator: pure AI beats people in the loop (S08, S09, S14, S21)
- **What:** human computers once filled buildings; a laptop spreadsheet replaced them. "would you want even one cell in your spreadsheet to be manually calculated?" (S08 65:31) His forecast: "companies that are entirely AI will demolish companies that are not." (S08 65:17)
  Once a machine is much better, a human override makes things worse, like elevators: "nobody wants an elevator operator because the automated elevator that stops at the floors is much safer than the elevator operator." (S14 22:22) Below human level, keep the human check (S14 23:41).
- **When:** any choice between hiring someone for digital work and building it with AI.
- **Score:** 8-10 for removing a manual step from a digital workflow once it has passed steps 1 to 3 of the algorithm and
  the AI beats the person on the error rate. 4-6 for AI with a human checker while it is still at human level. 1-3 for
  hiring a person to do keyboard-and-mouse work that AI can already do.

## 14. Zombie payment test: code, reason, owner (S20, S21, S22, T06)
- **What:** at the US Treasury he found payments going out with no category and no description: "Like basically untraceable blank checks." (S20 29:00)
  The fix was not a review board. Each payment needs a code, a comment and "someone to call about the payment" (S20 57:34),
  and "We're not judging the quality of the explanation, but there should be some explanation for what this payment is for above nothing." (S20 29:00)
  Recurring payments are the worst, like a card debit nobody reads the statement for (S21 126:16).
  Nobody on the receiving end flags it: "it's a pretty rare company or individual who will complain that they're getting money that they should not get." (S21 126:01)
  Aim for low, not zero: "you can manage to low fraud number, but not to zero fraud." (S21 135:12)
- **When:** any cost review, any "we need to cut 20%", any tool stack, contractor list or ad account.
- **Score:** 9-10 for an option that makes every recurring cost carry a reason and a named owner, then kills what has
  neither. 4-6 for a one-off audit with no standing control. 1-3 for across-the-board cuts that leave unowned spend alone.

## 15. Doers versus purchase-order writers (S23, S17)
- **What:** the Jose meme: one man digs, eight watch. "adding more people who, who simply, you know, issue purchase orders to Jose does not result in more things occurring, only m-if there are more Joses, more work." (S23 21:02)
  "So I aspire to be Jose." (S23 21:02) When the real builders are spread across too many projects, "the peanut butter is spread so thin you can barely taste it." (S23 22:06)
- **When:** hiring a manager, an account manager, a project coordinator, or a subcontractor who resells someone else's
  work; taking on more projects than the delivery team can carry.
- **Score:** a hire who does the work on the limiting factor scores 7-10. A hire whose job is to hand work to others
  scores 1-4 unless the doers are already full and the handoffs are the bottleneck. Taking new projects onto a team that
  is already spread thin scores low.

## 16. The Michael Jordan test before firing a star (S24, S09)
- **What:** delete-the-step is not delete-the-person. On a board ousting a founder CEO, he says "you need to think long and hard before kicking someone else like that out" (S24 03:47),
  "like kicking Michael Jordan off your team, um, when, when, when you, when you're on a winning streak" (S24 04:01). That CEO
  "does the roles of, like, several people." (S24 03:05) and many on his team turned down higher offers out of loyalty to
  him. The rule: "you have to balance the good that someone does and, and brings relative to the bad" (S24 04:01).
- **When:** firing a top performer who is difficult, made a costly mistake, or is expensive.
- **Score:** list every job the person holds together (clients, suppliers, people who stay because of them). If the list
  is long and the trend is winning, firing scores 1-3. If the person executes badly and the trend is not converging
  (framework 12), firing scores high.

## 17. Usefulness test (S06, S07, S13)
- **What:** value is how useful you are times how many people. "it's so hard to be useful, especially to be useful to a lot of people." (S06 22:38) A company should make its output worth more than the input (S07 113:02).
- **Score:** more useful to more customers scores high; extracting more money for the same value scores low.

## 18. Incentives predict behavior (S10, T06, S20, S21)
- **What:** "once you understand, un-un-understand the incentive structure, the behavior naturally follows." (S10 45:10) He leans on Milton Friedman:
  money "is most poorly spent when, when you're spending s- someone else's money on people you don't know" (S20 25:35).
  An open time-and-materials contract is a bad one, "which creates an incentive to turn molehills into mountains, as they never want to end the money train." (T06)
- **Score:** check what the option rewards. Fixed price and duration, owners per requirement, and short paths to the
  person doing the work score high; paying for hours, layers and spending other people's money with no owner scores low.

## 19. Coax the negative feedback (S11, S12, S16)
- **What:** his one piece of founder advice in 2012: "it's very important to, to seek out, uh, to actively seek out, um, and listen very carefully to negative feedback." (S11 21:00)
  Friends hide it, so ask for it: "Look, I d- don't tell me what you like. Tell me what you don't like." (S11 21:36) He takes it on camera, too (S16 01:27).
- **Score:** an option based on what unhappy customers said scores higher than one based on what happy ones said. A plan
  with no channel for bad news scores low.
