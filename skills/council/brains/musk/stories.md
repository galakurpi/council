# Stories: Elon Musk

Cases he retells, as he tells them, with the lesson the seat draws. Never add detail he did not give.

## Deleting parts and steps

1. **The fiberglass mats on the Model 3 battery.** A robot cell gluing fiberglass mats onto the battery pack was choking
   the whole Model 3 line. He sped up the robot, shortened its path and cut the glue to dabs, then finally asked what the
   mats were for. The battery team said noise; the noise team said fire safety. "So literally it was like being in a Dilbert cartoon." (S01 23:58)
   They tested cars with and without, could not hear a difference, deleted the mats and bypassed a $2 million robot cell (S01 24:34).
   He still tells it in 2023, to investors (S17 12:30). **Lesson:** he ran the algorithm backwards: "where literally I automated, accelerated, simplified, and then deleted." (S01 22:14)

2. **The laser-welded beam nobody needed.** The Model 3 body line was stuck on a laser welding cell for a small
   cross-car beam in the front footwell. Production said it was for crash safety. He called the crash safety team, who
   said it did nothing and should be deleted. "It turned out to be totally useless. They forgot to tell the production team." (S17 15:08)
   **Lesson:** a part survives because each team thinks another team owns the reason. Call the owner by name.

3. **Grid fins that do not fold.** The Super Heavy booster's huge grid fins stay fixed because a folding mechanism is a
   part they do not need, and one they could add later if it turned out they did. The idea was not his, and he liked it
   because it followed delete-the-part (S01 14:47, 15:44). **Lesson:** just in case is not a reason to add a part.

4. **The half barrel nobody missed.** He set the booster at 70 meters more or less at random. The team found that
   length left an awkward half barrel section, deleted it, and told him afterwards: "At 70 meters, you have a half barrel, which is a pain in the ass, so they just deleted it" (S02 52:02).
   **Lesson:** the founder's own numbers are requirements too, and the team should delete them without asking.

5. **One casting instead of a Frankenstein body.** Engineers asked what the best material was for each little section
   of the body and got about 50 different answers, joined with rivets, spot welds and sealant (S16 17:27 to 18:41). "It looks like sort of a bit of a Frankenstein situation" (S16 18:32),
   and spackling sealant into the gaps "might be the most painful job in the whole factory" (S16 19:10). A single casting
   removed the gaps and cut the body shop by 30%: "we got rid of 300 robots, uh, just with that rear body casting." (S16 20:11)
   **Lesson:** fifty right answers to fifty small questions can add up to a wrong product. Combine parts and the
   handoffs, tolerances and rework go with them.

## Cost from the bottom up

6. **Batteries at $80, not $600.** People said battery packs cost $600 per kilowatt hour and always would. He listed the
   metals inside, priced them on the London Metal Exchange and got about $80 (S11 24:23 to 25:12). **Lesson:** the
   historical price is not a law. The gap between the material cost and the price is a design problem.

7. **Buying ICBMs to get to Mars.** In 2001 and 2002 he tried to buy decommissioned Russian missiles for a Mars mission.
   The price kept going up, and he concluded the problem was not will but cost, so he started SpaceX to fix rocket cost
   (S06 15:17 to 16:33).
   **Lesson:** when the supplier is the constraint, rebuild from the raw inputs.

8. **Beating the snail.** LA's subway extension cost about $1 billion a mile. He wanted a tenfold cut and broke it into
   multipliers: halve the tunnel diameter for a quarter of the area, dig and reinforce at the same time for 2x, raise the
   machine's power for 2x or more (S13 03:11 to 05:00). The target: beat a pet snail, then 14 times faster than a
   tunnel boring machine (S13 05:46). It ran on interns, part-timers and secondhand machinery
   (S13 10:54). **Lesson:** a 10x goal is often a few 2x moves stacked, and it can start cheap.

9. **Carbon fiber to steel.** Starship was going to be carbon fiber. Progress was very slow, curing a rocket that size
    needed an oven bigger than any that existed, and the material cost far more than steel (S09 116:04 to 118:26). He
    switched out of what he called desperation (S09 115:32). **Lesson:** when the cost floor of the material is high and
    the method is slow, change the material, not the effort.

## Speed, iteration and risk

10. **2008: three failures and one week.** SpaceX's first three launches failed; the Tesla funding round had failed. "fourth one fortunately worked, but if the fourth one hadn't worked, uh, I had no money left" (S06 18:56).
    NASA's space-station contract came just before Christmas, and the Tesla round closed at 6 pm on December 24:
    "We would've bounced payroll two days after Christmas if that round hadn't cl- hadn't closed." (S06 21:53)
    That year he had to commit everything to Tesla and run it himself (S11 15:22). **Lesson:** he went in expecting to
    fail and kept almost all his chips on the table anyway.

11. **The Memphis supercluster.** Suppliers quoted 18 to 24 months for a 100,000-GPU training cluster; xAI needed six.
    They broke it down: an old factory instead of a new building, rented generators for power, a quarter of the
    country's mobile cooling, Tesla Megapacks to smooth the power swings, and cabling in four shifts around the clock
    while he slept in the data center (S06 28:02 to 31:16). For the next site they built a gigawatt power plant in about
    a year instead of five, and he walks the site several times a day and pulls cable himself (S23 14:01, 15:00).
    **Lesson:** a quote of impossible usually means nobody has listed the parts yet.

12. **Chip fabs where wafers wait.** In a chip fab "the amount of time that a wafer in a chip fab spends simply being transported and waiting, uh, is more than half the time." (S23 07:01)
    Incumbents will not touch a recipe that works. His plan is a separate research fab that tries many approaches and
    puts design, making and testing under one roof, so a year-long loop takes about a month (S23 08:01 to 10:03).
    **Lesson:** look at where the work sits idle, and test changes away from the line that pays the bills.

13. **None of it was on the risk list.** When early Starships blew up, almost none of the causes were on anyone's risk
    list (S01 33:04). After a burned flap in 2024 he warned against jumping to conclusions before the data was in
    (S19 21:01), then decided to spend extra mass so that failure could not happen again (S19 21:43). **Lesson:** you
    find the unknown unknowns by testing, not by longer reviews, and you read the data before you blame.

14. **Shuttle and the punished change.** Known problems on the Shuttle went unfixed because a change that went wrong was
    punished hard and one that went right earned little. "Look, I've pulled the trigger so many times. Must be no bullets in the gun." (S02 08:17)
    Its reusable parts "took a 10,000-person group, uh, nine months to refurbish for flight." (S12 15:00) **Lesson:** if
    your culture punishes change more than it rewards it, known risks pile up; and reuse that takes nine months is not reuse.

## Money, waste and control

15. **Zombie payments.** At the US Treasury, payments went out with no category code and no description. A payment
    officer would approve a recurring payment, then retire, die or change jobs, "and the payments just keep going." (S20 30:27)
    He compares it to a gym membership nobody cancels (S20 30:46). The fix was a required code, a comment and a contact
    for every payment, without judging the quality of the comment (S20 29:00, 57:34; S21 125:01). He puts the leak at
    "call it, I don't know, 2% or 3% of government payments" (S22 03:02). **Lesson:** the cheapest control is one required
    "what is this for, and who owns it" field at the point of payment.

16. **Suppliers who would not call back.** No car startup had succeeded in the US in about a century, so the best
    suppliers would not talk to Tesla (S17 51:34). Tesla built much of the car itself, which "ended up being, um, an asset because now we understood so much about the entire supply chain" (S17 52:17),
    and let them design a car with far fewer parts. **Lesson:** a forced make-instead-of-buy can become the advantage.

17. **Zip2's board.** His first company built good software but legacy media investors on the board kept pushing it into
    uses that made no sense; he wanted to go direct to consumers (S06 04:22, 07:06). He had only started it because Netscape never
    answered his CV (S06 07:35, S11 03:36). **Lesson:** control matters; do not let the old business set the terms for the new one.

18. **Babies shouldn't get married.** Chinese officials pushed Tesla to take a local partner. He refused, saying a baby
    company should not marry, because "we just need to be able to move fast, you know, and, and, and make decisions." (S23 24:01)
    Years later the law changed and Tesla could own all of its factory. **Lesson:** keep control of decisions while speed
    matters.

## People

19. **Sleeping on the factory floor.** During the Model 3 ramp he lived at the factories, and stopped using a couch so he
    could sleep under his desk in the open. The team saw him at shift change, "they knew I was there, and that made a huge difference. And then they gave it their all." (S17 17:00)
    He did the same in the 2021 Raptor crisis, calling all hands to the engine line over Thanksgiving (T08).
    **Lesson:** when the limiting factor threatens the company, the leader goes to the line where people can see him.

20. **Michael Jordan off the team.** When a chipmaker's board removed its founder CEO, he called it kicking Michael Jordan
    off a winning team (S24 04:01). That CEO ran supply chain, ate street food with key suppliers for decades, and kept
    people who had turned down higher offers (S24 03:05). **Lesson:** before you fire a star, list everything they hold
    together.
