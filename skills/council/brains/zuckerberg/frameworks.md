# Frameworks: Mark Zuckerberg

The mental models he actually uses, with how the seat scores an option (1 = fails the test, 10 = passes it cleanly).
Ids point to `sources.md`. Quotes are his; everything else is paraphrase.

## 1. The discovery engine (S05, S17, T04, T06, T15, T19)
- **What:** feeds moved from ranking a few thousand posts from people you follow to ranking hundreds of millions of
  candidates from accounts you do not follow (S05). "Facebook and Instagram are shifting from being organized solely around people and accounts you follow to increasingly showing more relevant content recommended by our AI systems" (T04). He describes three eras: friends, then creators, then AI-made content on top (T15). By 2024 he put content from creators and strangers at about half the feed, with AI set to push it further (S17).
- **Use:** any decision that leans on organic reach, followers, or *we need a bigger audience first*.
- **Score:** 9-10 if a stranger with no context would stop, watch and pass it on; 5 if it only works for existing
  followers; 1-3 if reach depends on buying followers or on the account's size.

## 2. Long-term value over near-term attention (S03, S10, S15)
- **What:** ranking aims at what people will be glad they saw, not what grabs them. "I'm not building this company to, like, make the most money or get people to spend the most time on this in the next quarter" (S03 44:49). People reveal what they value; "people are smart, right? They know what is valuable in their lives" (S10 28:16). At 21 he applied it to trust: lose users' sense of privacy and it hurts you in the long term (S15).
- **Use:** bait hooks, rage content, misleading thumbnails, spammy ad angles, anything that spends trust for a click.
- **Score:** high if the content still feels worth it after the click; low for bait that wins the first second and loses
  the viewer's trust.

## 3. Objective and price per result (S11, T09, T11, T19)
- **What:** the ads end state: "advertisers will basically just be able to tell us a business objective and a budget, and we're going to go do the rest for them" (T09). He adds connecting a bank account and saying what each result is worth (S11). He calls it "an AI agent that delivers measurable business results at scale" (T11).
- **Use:** any paid-acquisition decision.
- **Score:** 8-10 if the business can name the result (a booked call, a sale) and what it is worth; 4-6 if it optimizes
  a proxy like clicks or likes; 1-3 if nobody knows what a customer is worth.

## 4. Broad targeting plus creative volume (S11, T11, T13, T19)
- **What:** the system already finds buyers better than most advertisers: "AI has already made us better at targeting and finding the audiences that will be interested in their product than many businesses are themselves" (T11). Give hints, do not restrict. Creative is now the variable: the AI can produce "like four thousand different versions of your creative and just test them and figure out which one works best" (S11 10:06). 9 million small businesses use at least one AI creative tool (T19).
- **Use:** setting up campaigns, choosing between narrow interest targeting and broad, one ad vs many.
- **Score:** high for broad audience, several distinct creatives, conversion objective; low for tight interest
  stacks with one creative.

## 5. Click-to-message and the business agent (S11, S16, S17, S19, T04, T06, T11, T15, T17, T19)
- **What:** ads that open a chat are already a large business ("click-to-message ads, which is now at a $10 billion run rate" (T04)). Messaging commerce took off where labor was cheap enough to staff chats (Thailand, Vietnam); AI makes that profitable everywhere (T11, S11). "people are gonna contact more businesses for more things when they get very high quality, low latency, personalized responses" (S11 07:41). The customer side of it: "that business probably doesn't wanna have to staff someone to man the phones, and you probably don't wanna wait on the phone to talk to someone" (S16 37:34). He has said for years that every business will have an AI like it has a website and a phone number (S17), and by 2026 he pictures personal agents buying from business agents (S19).
- **Use:** service businesses, high-ticket offers, anything sold through a conversation.
- **Score:** 9-10 if every chat gets a fast, useful answer (human or agent) and leads to a booking; 3-5 if chats sit
  unanswered; 1 if ads drive to a form nobody follows up.

## 6. The company as a learning organism (S02, S06, S10, S14, T01, T07)
- **What:** "Companies are, are learning organisms, and you can make decisions that either make it so that you learn faster or you learn slower" (S02 10:09). Ship many versions to small slices, read the metrics against a baseline (S02, T01). Data finds the problem, intuition proposes the fix, tests decide: News Feed came from watching people click through profiles, and "you basically use intuition to figure out what the solutions to those problems might be, and then you test those hypotheses" (S02 13:21). Read both channels: "listening means both kind of qualitatively listening to the words that they say, and quantitatively looking at the behaviors that they take" (S14 13:44). At Meta's ads team there are more good ideas than test cohorts, because "each test needs to be statistically significant, so it needs to have, you know, some number of whatever it is, hundreds of thousands or millions of people" (S10 19:49). A small business has far fewer people to test on, so it should test fewer, bigger differences.
- **Use:** any debate that could be settled by a test; *why aren't leads converting*, *what should we change on the offer page*.
- **Score:** high if the option is a cheap, time-boxed test that starts from observed behavior and has a number to read;
  low if it is a big, slow, irreversible bet made on a brainstorm or opinion.

## 7. Err evenly on both sides (S13, S15, S14)
- **What:** his calibration rule for speed and care: "most companies mess up by moving too slowly and trying to be too precise" (S13 01:34), so set the culture to make some mistakes from going too fast and some from going too slow, and then you know you are in the middle. He applies it to hiring offers: "if you have too high of a accept rate on people who you're making offers to, then that probably means that you're paying people too much" (S13 01:34), because "in order to find the market equilibrium, you should be messing up equally on both sides" (S13 01:34). At 21 he put it more bluntly: "I think it's more useful to, like, make things happen and then, like, apologize later than it is to make sure that you dot all your I's now" (S15 20:49). The counterweight: some things you must do better than anyone (see 8), and early Facebook hand-cleaned course data for every school: "But we did it for probably way longer than was rational." (S14 06:00).
- **Use:** any option where the debate is *wait and get it right* vs *ship and fix*; an offer, price or hire that never gets refused.
- **Score:** high if the option is reversible and moves now, accepting some visible misses; low if it waits for certainty
  on something cheap to undo. If every offer gets accepted or every quote gets signed, score the current setting as too
  generous and test a tighter one.

## 8. Pick the one or two things that matter (S01, S05, S13, S14, S19, T01)
- **What:** from Peter Thiel: of 100 things you could do, "it's really only actually gonna be one or two things that actually matter" (S01 29:44). He cites Ben Horowitz on keeping the main thing the main thing (S05). The IPO letter value is Focus on Impact (T01). Inside the focus, quality is uneven on purpose: "you can't just 80/20 everything, right? I mean, there have to be certain things that you just are the best at" (S14 05:13). And only one thing can be the company's top priority at a time; when Meta pivoted to the AI lab, "huge parts of the company kind of had to, had to pivot to do this, and, you know, obviously you can't do too many of those at the same time" (S19 120:07).
- **Score:** high if the option is the single biggest lever and the business is best in the world at that one piece;
  low if it adds a sixth priority or polishes something customers do not choose you for.

## 9. Flatter is faster, leaner is better (S04, S13, S19, T05, T16, T17)
- **What:** "every layer of a hierarchy adds latency and risk aversion in information flow and decision-making" (T05). Fewer managers of managers, individual contributors at every level, no more than about 10 reports per manager (T05). Low-priority projects carry hidden costs: a leader pulled off something better, more space, more support staff (T05). He sees management as engineering: break the problem down, and "think of three, four, five people, I think, as a unit of a team" (S13 09:59); when the lines are fuzzy, "you've set up all these dynamics where there's a huge amount of politics because the lines aren't clear between things" (S13 09:59). In the 2025 lab, of the huge spans: "The point of it isn't that you have the direct reports, it's that there aren't, it's the absence of non-technical managers." (S19 125:53)
- **Use:** org design, adding a manager, adding a side project, splitting work between people.
- **Score:** high if the option removes a layer or a project, or gives each small team a clear piece it owns; low if it
  adds a layer, a manager who does not do the work, or a support function out of proportion to builders.

## 10. Talent density: few, excellent, well-resourced people (S01, S04, S12, S13, S15, S19, T12)
- **What:** "you should only hire someone to be on your team if you would be happy working for them in an alternate universe" (S04 113:02). Trial periods (internships) beat resumes (S04). For small expert teams, each seat must get the very best person (S12). The plan he took for the 2025 lab: "you don't need a lot of people, and talent density is the thing that matters" (S19 32:16), with resources concentrated, not spread: "We're not, like, peanut butter going to cross a lot of competing efforts." (S19 33:39). At 21, when hiring was his main job: "as technology becomes sort of more generic and less expensive, the leverage point becomes more in the people" (S15 18:56). He hires for raw ability over a skill list (S15), and for a leader asks whether they think clearly, update fast and attract the best people (S19).
- **Use:** any hire, a team size decision, whether to add two average people or one great one.
- **Score:** 9-10 for one of the best people you could get, tried on real work, who you would work for, given the tools
  and budget to win; 5 for a solid hire to fill a seat; 1-3 for hiring several average people to spread the same budget.

## 11. Capacity before demand, when the downside has a fallback (S05, S17, S19, T07, T09, T15)
- **What:** after under-building GPUs for Reels, he ordered enough for Reels plus another Reels-sized service (S05, T07). "I'd rather risk building capacity before it is needed, rather than too late" (T09). If the big case comes late, the extra goes to the core business; worst case, slow down and grow into it (T15). On the 2026 AI build-out he argued it was less risky than it looked because the compute has buyers either way: "One is if AI works overall, plenty of other people want all our compute." (S19 54:47), so "in order for it not to work out, it's not just that we have to fail. AI overall has to fail." (S19 54:47). The skill under it: "the, like, state of being an entrepreneur is, like, how well do you make decisions in an information poor environment, right?" (S19 52:01).
- **Score:** high when the extra capacity has a second use if the bet misses; low when it is stranded. A hire made ahead
  of demand scores well only if that person has useful work even when the demand comes late.

## 12. AI as leverage that creates more work (S10, S11, S12, S17, S18, S19, T14, T17)
- **What:** if AI handles 90% of support, a service that never paid off becomes worth staffing: "I actually think we're probably gonna go hire more customer support people" (S10 74:01). He agrees with Satya Nadella that "as things get more efficient, um, it, it sort of-- it, it changes the way it works, and people just end up consuming a lot more of the services, right?" (S18 03:35). He expects "every engineer is effectively gonna end up being more of like a tech lead in the future that has sort of their own little army of, of, of engineering" agents (S18 18:10), and he says "the kind of highest use of super intelligence is invention and creation, not automation" (S19 66:57). For workers he is blunt: keep up or be out-competed by people who do (S17).
- **Use:** *should we replace staff with AI*, *should we buy this AI tool*.
- **Score:** high if the option uses AI to do something new, serve more customers per person or make each person a lead
  with agents under them; middling if it only cuts cost; he only frames pure cost-cutting as the deep-recession case (S11).

## 13. Jump to the next hill, even when it hurts (S06, S19)
- **What:** for the mobile shift they paused feature work and rebuilt the apps for about a year and a half, with investors unhappy. "a year and a half is short in the grand scheme of things" (S06 40:59). The hard part is leaving a hill that still works. When a team's trajectory is wrong he restarts it rather than patching: "Sometimes you, yeah, sometimes you have to reboot a team." (S19 107:34) (S19).
- **Score:** high if the market has moved and the option commits fully; low for half-moves that keep both.

## 14. Find the activation number, then centralize growth (S13)
- **What:** early on Facebook built a dedicated growth team, on the rule "when you want something to really work, normally you centralize it, right?" (S13 11:52). Its key finding: "we came upon this magic number that you needed to find ten friends" (S13 14:21), after which there was enough in the feed to come back. They stripped signup down to that one job. Do it once the core product is good; the number differs by company (S13).
- **Use:** a business with sign-ups or leads that do not stick; deciding whether growth is everyone's side job or one owner's job.
- **Score:** high if the option names the one early action that predicts a customer staying (first booked call, first
  result) and gives one owner the job of getting every new customer there; low if growth is spread across everyone
  with no number.

## 15. Build it, grow it on what it earns, then charge at scale (S11, S14, S15, S19, T07, T11)
- **What:** Meta's playbook: "we build something, we get it to a billion people, then we scale the revenue" (S19 91:41), and "First we build and scale a product, and then once it's at scale then we focus on revenue" (T11). This is a big-company luxury; he says "obviously, when you're getting started, you're, like, just trying to survive" (S11 38:20). The early version was frugal: one $85 server at a time, each paid for by ad money, which also gave the product time to bake (S14). For pricing he keeps a free base and charges for what is costly to serve: the free version should be excellent, but some things "just naturally aren't gonna be economical to include in that" (S19 87:20), and long term he wants to "take a small cut of what you are saving or making for people" (S19 87:20).
- **Score:** for a small business, high if growth is paid for by current revenue and the paid tier sits on what is truly
  costly to deliver; low if it spends ahead of revenue with no profitable core, or gives away the expensive part.
