# Positions: Mark Zuckerberg

Where he stands on what comes up in a founder's decision. Paraphrased; quotes keep their source. Where the corpus says
nothing, it says so.

## Getting customers on Meta (the seat's home ground)

- **Organic content:** reach no longer needs followers. AI recommends content from accounts people do not follow, now a large and rising share of the feed (T04, T06, T15). Video is where time goes (T15, T17). He wants creators to treat Facebook as a home, calling it "this big arbitrage opportunity of being able to come reach the billions of people who are on Facebook" (S08 40:46). Make for one platform first: "where you have in mind as your home really matters" (S08 10:27).
- **What the feed rewards:** what people value over time and share, not bait (S03, S08). Ranking is moving to LLMs that understand "what the content is about and why it is compelling" (T19) and what each person is trying to achieve (T16, T17). So content that clearly serves a goal (learn, buy, fix a problem) should get easier to match to the right viewer.
- **Paid ads:** state the objective and what a result is worth; let the system find the audience; feed it creative (T09, T11, S11). "unless you really have a reason why you don't wanna sell to other people, don't constrain who we're gonna reach" (S11 11:06). A business with no creative will soon be able to start with only a goal (S11, S04).
- **Measuring ads:** he quotes Meta-commissioned research that "every dollar spent on our ads drives on average $3.31 in revenues for our advertisers in the US" (T06). Treat that as Meta's average, not a promise for one business. Ad gains he reports are conversion gains: "advertisers saw over 20% more conversions than in the year before" (T04), "It's driven roughly 5% more ad conversions on Instagram and 3% on Facebook" (T13).
- **Business messaging:** strongly for. Click-to-message ads, WhatsApp paid messaging, and now AI business agents (T04, T06, T15, T19). By mid-2026 agents ran on WhatsApp and Messenger for over a million businesses a week and were rolling out on Instagram, with summaries of what customers ask (T19). In 2026 he put an outside founder over WhatsApp to push commerce (S19).
- **Business AI agent:** every business will have one like it has a website and email (T09, T11, T13, S11, S17). He pictures it growing into "a business-in-a-box service that can help you start and run a whole business using Meta's platforms" (T19), and in 2026 described Muse as "making it like a business in a box for small businesses" (S19 89:12). Personal agents will buy from business agents, and more businesses ask to integrate than try to block it (S19).
- **Creators and one-person brands:** a creator's audience wants more of them than one person can give: "your community probably has a nearly unlimited demand to interact with you" (S17 33:58). His answer: an AI version, clearly labeled as AI (S16, S17).
- **Small vs large advertisers:** AI creative tools matter most for small budgets; agencies keep helping big brands use them (T13).
- **Shopping through agents:** shopping is the top Muse use case; he expects Meta to take a fee on transactions it helps close, paid by businesses (T20). He thinks agents grow the market, not just the conversion rate: "the opportunity here isn't just, okay, it's gonna be 20% more efficient on the conversion rate" (S19 76:16), and of businesses that stay out, "I think is gonna end up feeling sad over time" (S19 77:07).

## Hiring

- Hire only people you would be happy to work for (S01, S04). Bet on talent over experience; he started at 19 and promoted people who never ran a group before (S02, S11).
- Hire for raw ability over a skill list: someone with ten or fifteen years in an area can do that area, while a sharp generalist learns fast (S15). His best early people were young, often not CS majors (S15). Business skills alone were never a reason to hire; he sees scaling a sales force from five to a hundred people as the same kind of problem as scaling software (S15).
- Hiring was his main job at 21 with about 50 staff (S15); in 2025 he cleared his calendar for a month or two to recruit researchers himself (S19).
- Try people before committing: interns who did well over a summer are a better signal than resumes or short interviews (S04). New hires pair with the best people to learn how the team works (S15).
- Small expert teams beat big ones in frontier work; each seat needs the best person (S12), and resources go to the few rather than being spread (S19).
- Pay: if nearly every offer is accepted, you are probably overpaying; you should miss on both sides to find the market price (S13).
- For leaders: in a fast-changing field, pick a clear thinker who updates quickly and attracts excellent people over the expert in this year's technique (S19).
- Hiring is learned by mistakes, and the right profile changes by stage (S01).

## Firing and layoffs

- Layoffs are a last resort after other costs are cut; the leader takes the blame for over-hiring (T03). He laid off 11,000 in 2022, about 10,000 more in 2023 (T03, T05) and 8,000 in 2026 (T18).
- Be clear and early: in 2023 he announced the timeline months ahead because staff asked for more transparency (T05). Pay generous severance (16 weeks plus two per year of service, T03).
- He separates layoffs from performance: people leave "for reasons that aren't connected to their own performance" (S04 105:14).
- After the cuts, things moved faster than he expected (T05).
- When a team is on the wrong trajectory he will restart it under new leaders rather than tune it (S19), and after the Yahoo episode he replaced much of a management team that wanted the exit: "I ended up turning over a lot of the team after that" (S13 15:38).

## AI replacing people

- Mostly against the replacement framing. "I hear a lot of people out there talk about how AI is going to replace people." (T17), then argues AI amplifies people instead. He calls the job-loss focus a fixation (S11).
- His model: AI takes 90% of a task, the cost drops, so new services become worth doing and need more people (S10, S11). Cheaper means more consumed (S18).
- But he uses AI to shrink team sizes: "We're starting to see projects that used to require big teams now be accomplished by a single very talented person." (T16). He expected an AI mid-level engineer at Meta in 2025 (S07, T10) and, in April 2025, that "maybe half the development is gonna be done by AI as, as opposed to people" within about a year (S18 16:11).
- Each worker becomes a lead with agents under them (S18); everyone will build software by describing it (S17, S19). Workers who keep up will out-compete those who do not (S17).
- Net: use AI to raise output per person and serve more customers; cut layers, not ambition (T16, T17). He reserves pure cost-cutting for a deep recession (S11).

## Management and culture

- Flat: individual contributors at every level, managers with up to about 10 reports (T05, S04). Small core group with full context; few standing meetings; no recurring one-on-ones (S11). In the 2025 lab, the head of research had close to 200 direct reports; the point was having no non-technical managers, not managing 200 people (S19).
- Managers and leaders stay technical and in the details: use the agents, write code (S19). Product deciders should understand the technical trade-offs all the way down (S13).
- Structure as small teams of three to five with clear lines; unclear lines breed politics (S13).
- He does not delegate decisions he wants to be in (S11). "I think if you're gonna report to me, you need to be able to manage yourself." (S11 29:47) At 21 he signed off on every feature before launch (S15).
- Wants disagreement and ownership over blind execution (S19); likes a team that is optimistic about the whole and self-critical about its own project, and calls that healthy (S19).
- Keep builders as the main thing; support functions in proportion (T05, S04).
- In-person: Meta's data showed early-career engineers did better in person at least three days a week (T05).
- He thinks corporate culture had swung toward something too neutered and wants more of the intensity he found in martial arts (S07). On communication: "with communication, I think it's most important to just be authentic" (S19 132:01), even if some things come out wrong.

## Pricing and business model

- Free plus ads when you serve everyone (S03, S10). Pay products when the content or compute is costly (S10). Mixes of free, subscriptions and pay-for-results (T19, T20).
- Free base that is excellent, paid tier for what is costly to serve, and long term a small cut of the money the product makes or saves people (S19).
- For a small business's own pricing: no clear public position.

## Spending vs keeping cash, debt

- Spend heavily on long bets when the core is profitable (T05, T09, T15, S11). Survive first (S11, S01).
- Early Facebook did not spend money it did not have: one $85 server, ads to pay for the next one, growth gated by what revenue covered (S14). It gave the product time to bake while Friendster broke under fast growth (S14).
- Spend on making the product before paperwork: early on they skipped lawyers to build, and it cost some cleanup later, which he called the right use of time (S15).
- In a downturn, cut discretionary spend, freeze hiring, cut low-priority projects before cutting people (T03, T05).
- Build capacity early when the fallback use is clear (T09, T15, S19). Own the long-lived asset rather than rent it when you will use it for years; he counts owning data centers over paying cloud rates as an advantage (S19).
- Debt: no clear public position in this corpus, beyond noting that a profitable company with good credit can underwrite large investments (S19).

## Risk and speed

- Not taking risks is the biggest risk (T01, S02, S13). Move fast, ship rough, learn (T01, S06, S15, T20).
- Calibrate: miss as often from moving too fast as from too slow (S13).
- Make big moves when behind, without pride (S02 on Oculus; S11 on being late to Reels; S19 on rebooting the AI lab).
- Bold when others pull back: he wrote that in the new reality "most companies will scale back their long term vision and investments" (T05), and that Meta should be bolder instead.
- A launch usually needs a few rounds to find fit, and that is normal; a home run on day one is the exception (S19).

## Focus

- One or two things that matter (S01, S05, S13). In the downturn, fewer priority areas (T03). Only one thing can be the company's top priority (S19).
- Be the best at the few things customers choose you for and 80/20 the rest (S14).

## Selling, raising money and partners

- Do not start a sale process for a company you do not want to sell; when control changes, it will not be the same company (S13).
- Take money from people aligned with the long term (Peter Thiel as a founder-minded investor, Don Graham, Accel) and be skeptical of investors talking about flipping companies (S13).
- Selling can be right when the buyer wants to build what you want to build (S13).

## Agencies and services businesses

- Agencies: still useful, mainly for larger brands' creative and strategy (T13, S11). Small advertisers can do more themselves with AI tools (T13, S11).
- AI lets companies offload everything outside their core idea and stay small and talent-dense (S11).
- Businesses with a thin moat that try to block agents will be disrupted, he says: "But, but those aren't gonna... Those are gonna get disrupted." (S19 68:17)
- Running a services agency: no clear public position.
