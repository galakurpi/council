# Stories: Mark Zuckerberg

Cases he retells, as he tells them, with the lesson he draws. Quotes are his.

1. **Turning down Yahoo's billion.** In 2006, with about 10 million users, Yahoo offered around a billion dollars. Nearly
   the whole management team wanted to sell and, within a year, left. He blames himself for never explaining the
   long-term mission (S02, S06), and for the process itself: the only reason it became a famous story is that "we turned down a lot of money is frankly because I messed up the process, right?" (S13 15:38). He calls it one of his biggest management mistakes and says it showed him who was there for the exit (S13). Lesson: say where you are going before
   the hard trade-off arrives, do not open talks you do not want, and keep people who are in it for the long term.
2. **Under-building for Reels.** Recommending unconnected content meant ranking hundreds of millions of candidates
   instead of thousands, and Meta lacked the GPUs to catch TikTok as fast as it wanted. He then ordered enough for Reels
   plus another service of the same size (S05, T07). "where we kinda make some decision that ends up seeming good is because we messed something up before and just didn't wanna repeat the mistake" (S05 07:22). Lesson: buy buffer capacity for what you can't see yet.
3. **The mobile rewrite.** Meta paused feature development for about a year and a half to rebuild native apps and work
   out how ads fit inside a feed, with advertisers and users both uneasy (S06). Lesson: when the platform shifts,
   commit fully and accept the pain.
4. **Over-hiring after Covid.** He bet that the online surge would last, hired for it, and was wrong; 11,000 people
   went in November 2022 (T03). Later he wrote: "Since we reduced our workforce last year, one surprising result is that many things have gone faster." (T05) Lesson: own
   the forecast error, cut other costs first, then run leaner for good.
5. **Lockdown.** In 2005 a clone called College Facebook raced them school by school. Whenever a rival got ahead on
   something strategic, the team did not leave the house until it was fixed, a practice Meta still names (S01).
   Lesson: treat a competitor's lead on a strategic point as an emergency.
6. **The art-history study tool.** Behind on a final, he posted the course images on a site where classmates added
   notes; within an hour the whole class had filled it in (S01). In the 2012 telling, the professor said grades on that
   final had never been higher (S14). Lesson: build tools that channel a community's energy into a shared asset.
7. **How News Feed and Photos were found.** People kept clicking through friends' profiles to see what changed. First
   Meta showed who had updated, then built News Feed (S02). Photos came the same way: "what we observed was there was this behavior where a lot of people would every day upload a new profile picture" (S14 13:44), so once they had the servers and engineers they built full photo sharing (S14). Lesson: behavior shows the demand before customers name it; build the thing they are already hacking.
8. **Thailand and Vietnam.** Cheap labor let businesses there sell through chat, so those countries rank around 10th
   and 11th in Meta revenue while sitting in the 30s by GDP. AI agents should bring that model to rich countries (S11,
   T11). Lesson: when answering chats gets cheap, more selling happens in chats.
9. **The vesting mistake and the Florida LLC.** He did not know what a vesting schedule was when Peter Thiel required one; the equity
   split with a co-founder who never moved out turned into a fight. "that mistake probably cost me billions of dollars, but it's fine" (S01 20:15). The first company was set up wrong too: "we set it up as an LLC based out of Florida because that's where Eduardo happened to live at the time" (S13 05:47). Lesson: you will make mistakes; set up equity and structure properly once it is real, then move on.
10. **Customer support for 3.5 billion people.** Phone support was never affordable for a free service. If AI handles
    90% and hands off the rest, the cost drops to a tenth, and Meta would likely hire more support staff (S10, S11).
    Lesson: AI makes new services worth doing.
11. **Automating ad experiments.** A project on the ads team mined every past ranking experiment to suggest new ones.
    They found they already had more good ideas than compute or test cohorts to try them on (S10). Lesson: the bottleneck
    is clean tests, not ideas.
12. **Llama 4 off trajectory, and the reboot.** He assumed LLMs would scale like Meta's other machine learning; Llama 4
    fell short (S12, T12). Within weeks he was calling people for advice: "I was in this mode where I was just, like, asking a lot of different people what we should do" (S19 102:00). He then "cleared my calendar for, like, a month or two and was just meeting researchers and talking to them" (S19 103:13), hired Alexandr Wang, whose plan opened with talent density, and made it a full restart because the best recruits wanted something that felt new (S19). Wang had never trained a frontier model; he bet on a clear thinker the best people wanted to follow (S19). Lesson: in frontier work, talent density beats headcount, and the founder's own time is the recruiting budget.
13. **Small teams with AI ship fast.** "Muse went from an idea on a whiteboard to a successful product in just about 8 months" (T20). Shopping became the top use case, with purchases averaging over $200 (T20). He also wrote: "We're seeing more and more examples where one or two people are building something in a week that would have previously taken dozens of people months." (T17) He still expected Muse to need a few iterations to find fit (S19). Lesson: rebuild around the people who ship with AI, and plan for iteration even when you hope for a hit.
14. **Expanding school by school on $85 servers.** The first server cost $85 a month; ad money from Eduardo's deals paid for the next one, and growth was gated by that (S14). Facebook first launched at Yale, Stanford and Columbia because they already had campus networks: "I wanted to go to the schools that I thought would be the hardest for us to succeed at." (S14 10:58) After that, with little equipment, they chose the schools where students had the most friends already on the site, to get the most growth per server (S15). Lesson: test against the hardest market first, then grow as fast as revenue and capacity allow, and use the slow period to make the product solid.
15. **Ten friends.** Facebook's growth team found that a new user needed about ten friends before the feed had enough to bring them back, so they cut everything else out of signup to get people there (S13). Lesson: find the one early milestone that predicts a customer staying, and give one team the job of getting everyone to it.
16. **Real names did the moderation.** At 21 he contrasted Facebook with MySpace, where "MySpace has almost a third of their staff is monitoring the pictures that get uploaded for pornography" (S15 64:15), while Facebook got almost none because people used real names and school emails. He called it "a really simple social solution to a possibly complex technical issue" (S15 64:15). Lesson: before hiring people to police a problem, change the setup so the problem does not arise.
17. **The $100-200 billion check.** At the end of 2025, before the new models existed, Meta had to commit to compute and data centers for a product that did not exist yet: "we're gonna write this check for like, I don't know, like $100 billion- ... or $200 billion" (S19 53:18). His CFO warned that failure would be very bad financially; he argued the compute had buyers and other uses either way (S19). Then the agent took off and, in his own home, created demand rather than just converting it: "I went and got an Instacart account to give Muse, and now I use it like once or twice a week" (S19 76:10). Lesson: make the big bet when it has a fallback, and look for the new demand a new channel creates, not only the efficiency gain.
